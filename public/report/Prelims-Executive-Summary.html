Acknowledgements

The Bangsamoro Investment Roadmap Development (BIRD) 2026–2035 is the product of an integrated planning process that brings together economic analysis, investment facilitation, systems thinking, stakeholder perspectives, policy and institutional review, and performance-management design.

The preparation of this roadmap acknowledges the contribution of the Bangsamoro Government and its ministries, offices and agencies; local government units; development partners; business and investment stakeholders; academic and training institutions; civil society and community organizations; and practitioners whose information, experience and perspectives help strengthen the investment ecosystem.

Particular recognition is due to the institutions responsible for official statistics, economic planning, investment facilitation, infrastructure, trade, agriculture, environment, finance, human capital, tourism and local development. Their administrative and statistical systems provide the foundation for the transition from analytical findings to implementable programs, activities and projects.

The roadmap also draws on the BIRD validation survey and associated analytical architecture. The latest linked validation snapshot contains 76 consented responses. These responses are used as validation and diagnostic evidence rather than as a substitute for official administrative baselines or statutory targets.

The roadmap is designed to be strengthened through continued institutional validation, implementation learning and evidence-based recalibration.

Message from the Chief Minister

The Bangsamoro Investment Roadmap Development 2026–2035 presents a shared framework for turning the region's peace and autonomy dividend into durable economic opportunity. Its central proposition is clear: investment must be connected to productive capacity, infrastructure, human capability, environmental stewardship, market access and accountable institutions.

The roadmap therefore places ecosystem development at the center of investment policy. It recognizes the diversity of our provinces and communities while identifying the systems that connect them. It also emphasizes that growth must be resilient, inclusive and consistent with the values of ethical and responsible governance.

Implementation will require sustained cooperation among the Bangsamoro Government, local governments, national agencies, investors, financial institutions, development partners, civil society and communities. The BIRD provides a common direction; its success will depend on disciplined execution, transparent monitoring and continuous learning.

Executive Summary

The Emerging Bangsamoro: From Strategic Diagnosis to Investment Execution

The Bangsamoro Investment Roadmap Development (BIRD) 2026–2035 provides an evidence-informed strategic and implementation architecture for strengthening BARMM's investment ecosystem. It connects the regional context and diagnostic evidence in Chapters 1–2 with strategic options and leverage points in Chapter 3, KPI calibration in Chapter 4, strategy-map-to-investment-roadmap logic in Chapter 5, implementation and annual investment planning in Chapter 6, and policy recommendations in Chapter 7.

The central proposition is that investment performance depends not only on the volume of capital mobilized, but on the quality of the ecosystem that converts capital into productive capacity, jobs, market access, resilience and public value.

1. Purpose and Strategic Direction

Chapter 1 establishes the scope, rationale, principles, regulatory anchors, analytical frameworks and methodology of BIRD. The roadmap covers the Bangsamoro investment ecosystem over 2026–2035 and is designed as a decision and coordination framework rather than a substitute for project feasibility studies, statutory approvals, annual appropriations or agency mandates.

The analytical architecture combines the Bangsamoro Economic and Investment Ecosystem (BEIE) with systems thinking, TOWS strategy formulation, leverage-point analysis, KPI benchmarking and the Balanced Scorecard. This creates a continuous logic from context → diagnosis → strategy → measurement → execution → policy learning.

2. Diagnostic Findings: The Investment Ecosystem Must Be Treated as a System

Chapter 2 organizes BARMM's investment economy into five mutually dependent BEIE clusters: Foundations (agriculture, fisheries, forestry, energy and environmental assets); Transformers (processing, manufacturing, logistics and halal value addition); Enablers (infrastructure, digital systems, social services and human capital); Connectors (tourism, trade, exports, connectivity and BIMP-EAGA integration); and Financiers (Islamic banking, waqf, takaful, microfinance and investment capital).

The integrated quantified SWOT and systems diagnostics show that the principal challenge is not the absence of individual assets or opportunities. It is the interaction among infrastructure constraints, market access, standards and certification, institutional coordination, skills, finance, environmental risk and investor confidence. Systems archetypes such as Limits to Growth, Fixes that Fail, Growth and Underinvestment, Shifting the Burden and Tragedy of the Commons help explain why isolated interventions can produce temporary gains without durable system improvement.

Validation evidence. The latest linked validation snapshot contains 76 consented survey responses and uses analyticsVersion 2. Survey-derived SWOT diagnostics use the canonical formulas implemented in the validation-survey analytics function. Missing SWOT families are treated as missing observations, not zeros. These diagnostics inform prioritization and learning; official administrative baselines and targets must be verified by their authoritative data owners.

The cluster-level diagnostics provide a disciplined basis for identifying leverage points, but they should be interpreted as decision-support evidence rather than as a substitute for official administrative statistics. This distinction is central to the roadmap's measurement governance.

3. Strategy: Four Options, One Integrated Strategic Architecture

Chapter 3 converts diagnosis into strategic choice. It formulates four strategic options—Halal Economy Dominance Strategy (HEDS), Green Economy Monetization Strategy (GEMS), Infrastructure-First Enabling Strategy (IFES), and Integrated Ecosystem Development Strategy (IEDS)—and evaluates them using a seven-criteria analytical decision aid covering strategic alignment, economic impact, social inclusion, environmental sustainability, feasibility, risk mitigation and moral-governance alignment.

IEDS serves as the organizing ecosystem strategy that connects the options rather than reducing the roadmap to a single-sector intervention. The strategy is reinforced by five Critical Leverage Points: LP1 Halal Certification System Integrity; LP2 Infrastructure–Energy–Connectivity Nexus; LP3 Governance–Investor Confidence Feedback; LP4 Islamic Finance Ecosystem; and LP5 Green Economy Revenue Framework.

The strategic logic is therefore portfolio-oriented: halal value chains, green economy activation, infrastructure and connectivity, finance, governance, skills and market access are mutually reinforcing components of one investment ecosystem.

4. Measurement: From Indicators to Governed Performance

Chapter 4 establishes the BIRD measurement architecture. It uses six Pareto-style headline KPIs as a high-signal executive shortlist: annual investment approvals, BARMM GRDP, exports, halal-certified MSMEs, electrification and broadband connectivity. The term “Pareto-style” is intentionally used as a management designation and does not claim that six indicators cause a fixed share of total BIRD impact.

5. Balanced Scorecard: Translating the Strategy Map into an Investment Roadmap

Chapter 5 is the strategic management bridge between the strategy architecture and the implementation system. The BSC translates the Strategy Map into an Investment Roadmap by specifying how capabilities enable processes, processes create stakeholder value, and stakeholder outcomes contribute to financial and economic results.

The four perspectives retain their causal order: Learning & Growth → Internal Process → Stakeholder → Financial. The three-phase implementation architecture described in Chapter 6 is not the roadmap itself; it is the implementation framework used to sequence delivery according to readiness, dependencies and investment capacity.

6. Implementation Framework: Three Phases, Not Three Roadmap Alternatives

Chapter 6 converts the strategy and scorecard into programs, activities, projects, governance arrangements, a one-year action plan and MEL processes. The three phases are the implementation framework for sequencing the Investment Roadmap:

Phase 1 — Foundation Building (2026–2028). Strengthen governance and investor confidence; operationalize halal certification and standards alignment; address priority infrastructure and connectivity constraints; launch digital facilitation; establish coordination protocols and priority PAPs.
Phase 2 — Acceleration (2029–2031). Scale value-adding production, halal processing, green-economy programs, Islamic finance, logistics, cold-chain and market-access systems as enabling conditions mature.
Phase 3 — Consolidation and Global Integration (2032–2035). Consolidate mature systems, deepen BIMP-EAGA and international market integration, strengthen green and halal market positioning, and institutionalize performance and investment facilitation systems.

The indicative decade-wide investment envelope is ₱120–160 billion. It remains subject to project readiness, feasibility, safeguards, financing availability, annual appropriations and applicable approvals. The Year 1 action plan identified in Chapter 6 lists approximately ₱2.063 billion of programmed spending, with the ZBIP component accounting for the overwhelming majority of that listed amount.

7. BIF-Net and Execution Governance

Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 establish BIF-Net as the investment facilitation and coordination layer. It is not a replacement for existing ministries, offices, LGUs or statutory mandates. Its purpose is to improve shared visibility of investment opportunities, investor issues, PAP status, KPI performance and cross-agency dependencies.

The execution model combines role-based information views, KPI monitoring, action tracking and adaptive-management protocols. Data architecture should preserve the distinction between survey-derived validation evidence, official administrative data and management targets. This prevents analytical estimates from being silently converted into official baselines.

8. Policy Recommendations and the Way Forward

Chapter 7 converts the roadmap into policy and institutional recommendations under four broad areas: policy and institutional enablers; planning, budgeting and territorial delivery; investment mobilization and market development; and governance of the way forward.

  • Institutional enablement: strengthen the policy, standards, governance and coordination conditions required for investment facilitation, halal development, green-economy activation and investor confidence.
  • Territorial delivery: align infrastructure, value chains, human capital and investment facilitation with provincial and geographic differences rather than applying a uniform intervention model.
  • Investment mobilization: develop credible, feasible and market-oriented investment opportunities supported by aftercare, information, standards, infrastructure and financing pathways.
  • Governance and learning: maintain target traceability, evidence quality, adaptive review and cross-agency accountability through BIF-Net and the MEL architecture.

Across these recommendations, the roadmap treats ethical governance, resilience, inclusion and peace as operating conditions for investment—not as parallel themes detached from economic performance.

9. The Investment Logic in One Chain

Context and assets → BEIE diagnosis → quantified SWOT and systems sensing → strategic options and leverage points → Strategy Map → Investment Roadmap objectives and KPIs → implementation framework and PAPs → MEL and adaptive management → policy learning and reinvestment.

This chain is the core coherence mechanism of BIRD 2026–2035. Each chapter has a distinct role: Chapter 1 establishes the frame; Chapter 2 explains the system; Chapter 3 defines strategic choices and leverage; Chapter 4 defines measurement and calibration; Chapter 5 translates the Strategy Map into the Investment Roadmap; Chapter 6 sequences and manages implementation; and Chapter 7 establishes the policy and institutional conditions for continuity.

10. Conclusion

The BIRD 2026–2035 is designed to help BARMM move from fragmented investment interventions toward a coordinated investment ecosystem. Its emphasis is not simply on attracting more capital, but on improving the conditions under which capital becomes productive, inclusive, resilient and sustainable economic activity.

The roadmap's success will ultimately depend on institutional ownership, investment readiness, credible implementation, reliable data, transparent governance and continuous learning. The document therefore provides a framework for coordinated action while preserving the authority of competent institutions to validate baselines, approve projects, allocate budgets, regulate activities and revise priorities as evidence and conditions change.

The roadmap provides the architecture; implementation, institutional learning and the Bangsamoro's collective choices will determine the outcomes.
