Strategic Options, Leverage Points and the Roadmap Strategy

Introduction: From Diagnosis to Strategy

Chapter 2 diagnosed the Bangsamoro economy as one connected system. It quantified 55 strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats across the cross-cutting operating systems and the five clusters of the Bangsamoro Economic and Investment Ecosystem (BEIE) Framework; it identified the systems archetypes that keep the region's investment performance below its potential; and it showed that readiness gaps compound along the value chain, so that clusters that look about 69 percent ready on average deliver only about 11.5 percent of their potential as a system. This chapter turns that diagnosis into a strategic choice.

It does so through three analytical layers, each building on the one before:

  1. SWOT-to-strategy conversion (Section A, A.1–A.4). The quantified SWOT register is converted into SO, WO, ST and WT strategies using the TOWS matrix (Weihrich, 1982). Every strategy is traced to scored survey items, the BEIE cluster that owns it and the archetype it addresses.
  2. Systems-archetype-informed leverage points (Section B). Each archetype is mapped to the levels of Donella Meadows' hierarchy of places to intervene in a system (Meadows, 1999), and the results are consolidated into the five Critical Leverage Points (LP1–LP5) used in Chapters 4 to 7.
  3. Multi-criteria evaluation of four strategic options (Section A, A.5–A.7). Halal Economy Dominance (HEDS), Green Economy Monetisation (GEMS), Infrastructure-First Enabling (IFES) and Integrated Ecosystem Development (IEDS) are scored against seven weighted criteria, tested for sensitivity and then compared with stakeholder validation results.

Section A

Systems-Based Strategy Matrix and Strategic Options

The TOWS matrix (Weihrich, 1982) pairs internal factors (strengths and weaknesses) with external factors (opportunities and threats) to generate four families of strategy. BIRD applies it with three disciplines borrowed from systems thinking. First, only scored items enter the matrix: each pairing uses factors from the BIRD Validation Survey register rather than descriptive lists. Second, each strategy is assigned to the BEIE cluster that owns its delivery, so that the matrix can be read as a map of the ecosystem. Third, each strategy is tagged with the archetype it is designed to weaken and the Meadows level at which it intervenes, which links Section A to Section B.

Scoring conventions. SWOT items use the canonical Strat Planner Pro formulas applied in Chapter 2: Strength Resilience Index (RI) = (I × L) / 5; Opportunity RI = √(I × L); Weakness Risk = I × L on a 1–25 scale; Threat Vulnerability Index (VI) = (I² × L) / 25, where I is impact and L is likelihood, each rated 1–5. The four families therefore sit on different scales and are compared within, not across, families.

A.1 SO Strategies: Using Strengths to Capture Opportunities

SO strategies are the growth pathway. They connect the region's strongest scored assets (a large resource base, seaweed and agricultural specialisation, cultural credibility and location) with its highest-rated external opportunities (halal markets, renewable investment and regional integration). They are attractive but conditional: each depends on Enablers that Chapter 2 found to be the weakest cluster.

SO-1 is the core of the halal pathway. Its strongest pairing is not halal legitimacy itself, which scores in the middle of the strength register (3.26), but the resource base that halal products are made from: seaweed (3.71) and agriculture, fisheries and forestry (3.69), matched with the highest-rated opportunity in the register, the global halal market (4.13). The constraint is conversion, not supply: Tawi-Tawi exports most of its seaweed raw, and cold chain (16.42) and market linkage (16.41) are among the highest-rated weaknesses. SO-1 therefore intervenes at L10 (processing, testing and cold-chain capacity built ahead of demand) and at L5 (accreditation rules that open export markets), anchored by the Bangsamoro Halal Park in Matanog and agro-industrial corridors in Lanao del Sur and the SGA (MTIT, 2025; MTIT, 2026).

SO-2 converts environmental endowments into revenue through renewable energy, payment for ecosystem services (PES) and, where eligible, carbon markets. Its legal precondition is the Bangsamoro Forestry Code and the joint MENRE–MILG issuance on benefit sharing, both still pending. Without these rules, SO-2 would accelerate the Tragedy of the Commons rather than resolve it. SO-3 uses location and culture to reach BIMP-EAGA and Muslim-friendly tourism markets; it intervenes in physical links (ports, the Mindanao–Sabah landbridge concept, island logistics) and in rules that spread benefits beyond the already-connected mainland.

A.2 WO Strategies: Using Opportunities to Overcome Weaknesses

WO strategies are turnaround initiatives. They matter most in BARMM because the register's highest-risk items are internal: six of the ten highest-rated weaknesses sit in the Enablers cluster. External opportunities, such as concessional climate finance, digital technology and Islamic finance, supply the capital and capability that internal resources alone cannot.

WO-1 addresses the physical ceiling on growth. Household electrification reached 67.52 percent region-wide at the end of 2025, against a Bangsamoro Sustainable Energy Master Plan target of 90 percent by 2035 and 100 percent by 2040; broadband coverage is estimated in the BIRD KPI register at below 30 percent (MENRE-BARMM, 2024; Context.ph, 2026). The ₱6.67 billion Zamboanga–Basilan Interconnection Project is the clearest example of a single enabling asset that changes what Basilan can process (MTIT, 2026). WO-1 intervenes mainly at L10 (building the stock of roads, power and connectivity), L9 (shortening project preparation and delivery delays) and L11 (reserve capacity so that new investment does not immediately saturate the grid).

WO-2 addresses the highest-rated risk in the entire register. In 2024 only 64.7 percent of BARMM residents were functionally literate, against 70.8 percent nationally, and basic literacy (81.0 percent) was the lowest of any region (PSA, 2025). Because human capital accumulates slowly, WO-2 must start in Phase 1 even though its returns arrive mainly in Phases 2 and 3; this is the classic delay that makes capacity investment easy to postpone. WO-3 builds the conversion capacity that SO-1 depends on, and WO-4 builds Islamic financial capability (products, providers and financial literacy) rather than relying on one-off conventional or grant financing, the pattern that Chapter 2 identified as Shifting the Burden.

A.3 ST Strategies: Using Strengths to Absorb Threats

ST strategies are protective. They deploy the region's strengths against the threats that could reverse investment gains. The threat register is led by security incidents (VI 3.20), commodity price volatility (3.19) and climate change (3.14), all external pressures that BARMM cannot remove but can buffer.

ST-1 rests on a realistic reading of competition. Established halal hubs in Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand have deeper supply chains and recognised certification systems. BARMM's route to premium markets is therefore not price but trust: certification that importing authorities accept, backed by laboratory testing and traceability. This makes accreditation and mutual recognition under OIC/SMIIC standards a critical-path item for the halal pathway rather than a compliance formality (SMIIC, 2021; MTIT, 2025).

ST-2 treats climate risk as a present cost. El Niño-related drought led Maguindanao del Sur to declare a state of calamity in 2024, and Pestalotiopsis leaf disease has affected at least 800 hectares of rubber in Basilan (Chapter 2). The strategy combines buffers (irrigation, crop diversification, climate-resilient varieties), information (early warning and climate-risk screening of projects) and rules (adaptation conditions on subsidies), avoiding relief-only responses that recreate the problem. ST-3 uses the peace dividend, including Basilan's declaration as free of the Abu Sayyaf Group on 9 June 2025 (PIA, 2025), to strengthen the balancing loops that keep isolated incidents from becoming investor-wide risk premiums: rapid incident response, verified security information for investors and protection of project sites.

A.4 WT Strategies: Reducing Weaknesses and Avoiding Threats

WT strategies address the points where internal weakness meets external threat, and where the region is most exposed. They are defensive in form but preventive in purpose: they protect the roadmap itself from the failure modes Chapter 7 identifies, namely being ignored after a change of government, executed in silos or allowed to drift.

WT-1 is the cheapest source of additional capital in the roadmap. Underspending is rated a high risk (16.85), and every percentage point of budget execution recovered is capital that does not have to be raised. The strategy works through information (one version-controlled baseline and project register, as recommended in Chapter 7, B1), rules (readiness gates and published service standards) and goals (protecting targets from being lowered quietly when performance lags). Respondents rated the Drifting Goals archetype accurate at 98.6 percent, and 30 of 45 who answered the follow-up said both of its loops are equally observable (BIRD Validation Survey, 2026).

WT-2 introduces cost and schedule assurance, because infrastructure cost overruns carry the highest impact rating of any threat (I = 4.28). WT-3 resolves the land-tenure overlaps (ancestral domain claims, private titles and public land) that block agro-industrial siting, with free, prior and informed consent where indigenous peoples' rights apply. WT-4 responds to the first elected Bangsamoro Parliament: the roadmap must be anchored in law, budget rules and published targets so that it outlasts any single administration (Chapter 7, D1).

A.5 Strategic Options Formulation

The fourteen TOWS strategies can be bundled in different ways, and each bundle expresses a different theory of how BARMM's economy should grow. Four coherent options were formulated during the BIRD workshops and tested in the validation survey. The options are not mutually exclusive in content; they differ in emphasis, sequencing and the constraint they treat as binding.

Option profiles

Option 1 · Halal Economy Dominance Strategy (HEDS)
Thesis. Use the region's halal legitimacy and resource base to build certified, processed and exported halal products, with the Bangsamoro Halal Park, Polloc Freeport and provincial agro-industrial corridors as anchors. Strengths. The strongest identity fit and the clearest investor narrative. Weakness. It concentrates on the Transformers cluster while the binding constraint lies in Enablers; it also exposes the region to the threat of competition from mature halal hubs (VI 2.88) before certification is internationally accepted.
Option 2 · Green Economy Monetisation Strategy (GEMS)
Thesis. Turn forests, watersheds, marine ecosystems and renewable resources into revenue through renewable energy, PES, eco-tourism and eligible carbon instruments. Strengths. The highest sustainability score and a direct response to the Tragedy of the Commons. Weakness. The thinnest institutional base: the Forestry Code and the benefit-sharing issuance are pending, measurement, reporting and verification (MRV) systems are not yet built, and community safeguards must precede any transaction.
Option 3 · Infrastructure-First Enabling Strategy (IFES)
Thesis. Remove the binding infrastructure, energy and human-capital constraints first, then scale production. Strengths. It targets the cluster that Chapter 2 identified as binding and has high systems leverage. Weakness. It is sequential: by deferring conversion and market capacity, it leaves returns invisible for years, which weakens the political and fiscal case for continuing investment.
Option 4 · Integrated Ecosystem Development Strategy (IEDS)
Thesis. Synchronise the strongest elements of Options 1 to 3 according to their dependencies: Enablers and Foundations are prepared first, Transformers scale as their prerequisites are verified, and Connectors and Financiers deepen as product and project flows grow, with the operating systems working throughout. Strengths. It is the only option consistent with the serial-throughput finding of Chapter 2 and the only one that addresses every diagnosed archetype. Weakness. It is the most demanding to coordinate, which is why its feasibility depends on the orchestration arrangements set out in Chapters 6 and 7.
Why serial throughput favours synchronisation. When value must pass through several clusters in sequence, system output is the product of their readiness levels, not the average. In a product, a one percent improvement in any cluster raises total throughput by about one percent, so no cluster can be safely deferred; and because the absolute gain is largest where readiness is lowest, the weakest link (Enablers) must be raised first. A single-sector option improves one factor of the product and leaves the others unchanged.

Three readings follow. First, IEDS leads on five of the seven criteria and is never ranked below second. Second, each single-sector option leads where its thesis predicts: HEDS on identity alignment, GEMS on sustainability (tied with IEDS) and IFES on systems leverage among the standalone options. The lowest score for IEDS is risk–return (7.5): integration spreads exposure across more fronts, a cost that the phase gates in Section A.7 are designed to manage.

Reading the validation evidence

The order is fully reproduced; the conviction is not. Stakeholders reproduced the baseline order exactly, in the full sample and in the subset who moved at least one slider. However, their scores are lower and closer together: the IEDS lead over HEDS is 0.15 points in the full sample and 0.22 among slider movers, against 1.30 points in the baseline. At the level of individual respondents, IEDS was the strict first choice of 22 percent of slider movers (32 percent including ties). The baseline should therefore be read as a well-supported direction, not as a decisive margin of stakeholder preference.

Default responses compress the spread. Twenty-five of the 75 respondents who reached the matrix left all 28 cells at the default value of 5. Their answers pull every option towards the midpoint, which is why the slider-mover subset is reported alongside the full sample.

Where stakeholders see it differently. Among slider movers, HEDS outscored IEDS on identity alignment (6.74 against 6.58), and inclusivity was IEDS's strongest criterion (7.06). Stakeholders thus see the integrated strategy as the more inclusive choice and the halal strategy as the more authentic expression of Bangsamoro identity. IEDS should carry the halal agenda visibly, rather than subsume it.

A.7 Recommended Strategy: The Integrated Ecosystem Development Strategy (IEDS)

The Integrated Ecosystem Development Strategy is recommended as the overarching strategy for BIRD 2026–2035. Three arguments support the recommendation, each independent of the others:

  1. Structural fit. The economy behaves as a serial chain whose output is capped by its weakest cluster. Only an option that raises all clusters in dependency order can lift system throughput materially (Chapter 2, Section G).
  2. Archetype coverage. IEDS is the only option that responds to all six cluster archetypes and the three governance archetypes diagnosed in Chapter 2 (Section B.2).
  3. Robustness. IEDS ranks first under every weighting tested and in both stakeholder samples, and was endorsed outright or partially by 64 of 65 respondents.

IEDS does not mean doing everything at once. It means testing each project against the upstream conditions it needs (reliable power and logistics, lawful land access, capable workers, accepted standards, finance, safeguards and credible offtake) and funding those conditions alongside the project where justified. The sequence is implemented through the three phases that Chapter 6 operationalises.

The budget-priority tension. Asked where budget should concentrate, respondents put Foundations first (29 of 71) and the operating systems second (15), with Enablers fifth (6), even though readiness data identify Enablers as the binding cluster. The two views are reconciled rather than overruled: Enablers investments are selected and justified by the Foundations pipelines they unlock, such as farm-to-market roads, cold chain and power for agro-processing (Chapter 7, B4).

Financing logic and 2035 targets

The ₱120–160 billion envelope is an indicative requirement for public and catalytic finance over the decade, not a single approved budget. Chapter 6 sets out an illustrative financing scenario of 35 percent from BARMM appropriations and other regional public resources, 25 percent from national government programmes, 30 percent from official development assistance and climate finance, and 10 percent from private capital, public–private partnerships and Islamic finance. Stakeholders broadly support blended finance (63 of 72 agree or strongly agree), but rated the fairness of the proposed mix more cautiously (3.68 of 5). Each share must be replaced by verified appropriations or signed commitments as they become available.

Systems Archetype-Informed Strategic Leverage Points

Section A chose the strategy; Section B identifies where, within the system, that strategy should push. A leverage point is a place in a system where a small, well-placed change produces a large and lasting shift in behaviour (Meadows, 1999). Meadows ranked twelve such places from the least to the most powerful, and the hierarchy is used here as a practical diagnostic: for each archetype diagnosed in Chapter 2, it asks which level of intervention would weaken the structure that produces the problem.

B.1 Identifying Leverage Points: A Three-Step Diagnostic Process

Leverage points were identified through a three-step process from the Strat Planner Pro architecture, applied first in the regional and provincial workshops and then tested in the validation survey (Silva, 2026; BoI-MTIT, 2026).

Step 1: Diagnostic synthesis. Recurring patterns are identified from the scored SWOT register and the workshops: production that cannot scale beyond local power and logistics limits; certification and processing capacity that lags demand; shared resources degraded by fragmented governance; investment concentrated in the mainland corridor; and short-term financing that delays durable institutions.

Step 2: Loop mapping. Each pattern is traced to its feedback structure. Chapter 2 mapped the investment–development reinforcing loop and the governance–investor confidence loop (rated accurate by 97 and 91 percent of respondents) and the balancing loops that cap them. The question at this step is where a loop is delayed, broken or dominated by a balancing limit.

Step 3: Tiered selection. Interventions are chosen across realms. Parameter changes are fast but shallow; design and intent changes are slow but durable. A credible portfolio uses both, with deeper interventions protecting the gains of shallower ones. Respondents found the leverage points clear (mean 4.00 of 5; 76.7 percent agree) and agreed that activating them would accelerate the ecosystem (4.03; 79.5 percent).

Activating leverage points

The archetype map shows a clear pattern. Four levels recur across almost every archetype: L10 (building the stock of physical and human capacity), L6 (making performance visible), L5 (changing the rules that shape behaviour) and L3 (protecting the goals). BARMM's past interventions have concentrated on L12 and L10, through budget amounts, incentive rates and infrastructure projects. These remain necessary, but without the design and intent levels they are vulnerable to Fixes that Fail and Drifting Goals. IEDS therefore pairs every major capital programme with a rule, an information flow and a goal that protect it.

Design principle. No major investment without its protecting rule. For example: a processing facility (L10) is paired with an accreditation pathway (L5) and published certification times (L6); an energy interconnection (L10) with a tariff and dispatch arrangement (L5) and a reliability dashboard (L6); a provincial allocation (L12) with an equity floor (L5) and a provincial disparity indicator (L3, L6).

B.4 Prescription: Front-Loading the Ecosystem Enablers

The first prescription addresses the Limits to Growth and Growth and Underinvestment archetypes. BARMM's growth has repeatedly stalled not for want of opportunity or investor interest, but because power, logistics, certification and skills could not support production at scale. The prescription is to raise capacity ahead of demand, so that the ceiling moves before growth reaches it. Respondents agreed that capacity traps are a real constraint (mean 3.99 of 5; 74.0 percent agree).

Two enabler tracks run in parallel from Phase 1. The hardware track (L10, L11) covers energy interconnection and renewable mini-grids, including completion of the Zamboanga–Basilan Interconnection Project; farm-to-market roads in the production corridors of Maguindanao del Sur, the SGA and Lanao del Sur; port and island logistics for Tawi-Tawi and Basilan; cold-chain nodes; and digital connectivity under the Bangsamoro e-Governance Master Plan (MICT, 2025). The software track (L9, L10) covers functional literacy and industry-aligned technical and vocational training; certifiers, laboratory analysts and investment-facilitation officers recruited and trained before demand peaks; and shorter preparation and approval times for priority projects.

The test for every Phase 1 project is not only whether it is worthwhile in itself, but which downstream investment it unlocks. Basilan illustrates the point: improved power supply and the province's declaration as free of the Abu Sayyaf Group change what its 48,366 hectares of rubber can support, from raw cup-lump sales towards processing and rubber-wood products, but only if processing, logistics and skills follow in the same sequence.

B.5 Prescription: Going Below the Waterline and Architecting Collaborative Governance

The second prescription works at the deeper levels of the hierarchy. The iceberg model distinguishes visible events from the patterns, structures and mental models beneath them; intervening only at the level of events produces short-term fixes (Senge, 1990). Respondents strongly endorsed this framing (mean 4.26 of 5; 85.1 percent agree), and agreed that collaborative governance is essential to the roadmap (4.08; 80.6 percent).

LevelWhat it looks like in BARMMIEDS response

Collaborative governance resolves three governance traps together. Big Man dynamics are weakened by institutionalising decisions: merit-based appointments, transparent procurement and open budget data (L5, L6), and by a shift from authority exercised over others to authority exercised with them (L2). Escalation is weakened by shared superordinate goals, such as the halal hub and BIMP-EAGA integration, that make cooperation between provinces more valuable than competition (L2), and by transparent, formula-based allocation (L5). Success to the Successful is weakened by explicit equity goals and a proposed allocation floor for lagging and island areas, subject to legal design (L3, L5; Chapter 7, B3).
