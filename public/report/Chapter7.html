Policy Recommendations and the Way Forward

From Strategy to Coordinated Action: Policy, Institutional and Investment Priorities for the BIRD 2026–2035 Roadmap

Chapter Purpose

Chapter 7 converts the preceding analytical architecture into a policy agenda. It does not introduce a new strategy. It identifies the institutional, regulatory, planning and investment actions required to carry the diagnostics of Chapter 2, strategic options and leverage logic of Chapter 3, KPI calibration of Chapter 4, Balanced Scorecard and causal architecture of Chapter 5, and implementation system of Chapter 6 into sustained execution.

Operating principle: policy recommendations should be treated as coordinated actions within the BEIE system, not as isolated sectoral initiatives. The Bangsamoro Investment Facilitation Network (BIF-Net) is the cross-cutting facilitation and coordination mechanism referenced in the implementation architecture.

Evidence, measurement and target-governance note. The latest linked validation snapshot contains 76 consented survey responses and uses analyticsVersion 2. Survey-derived diagnostics are stakeholder-validation evidence for prioritization and learning; they are not automatically official administrative baselines. The canonical SWOT formulas are those implemented in the validation-survey analytics function, and missing SWOT families remain missing observations rather than zeros. Official baselines, targets, budgets, investment approvals, GRDP, exports, poverty, connectivity and other administrative indicators should be verified with their authoritative data owners before formal adoption or public reporting.

A. Policy and Institutional Enablers

The first policy task is to strengthen the institutional conditions under which the roadmap can be implemented. Chapter 2 identified fragmentation, infrastructure constraints, market-linkage gaps and governance challenges across the BEIE clusters; Chapter 3 translated these findings into leverage points and strategic options; and Chapters 4–6 established the measurement, scorecard and implementation architecture. The recommendations below therefore focus on enabling conditions rather than creating parallel programs.

Recommendation 7.1: Operationalize the Bangsamoro Forestry and Green-Economy Framework

Primary actors: Bangsamoro Parliament; Chief Minister's Office; MENRE; relevant LGUs and environmental institutions.

Action: Advance the applicable forestry and natural-resource policy framework, establish clear rules for community participation and benefit sharing, and develop implementation protocols for Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES), restoration, sustainable forest management and other eligible ecosystem-service mechanisms.

Rationale: Chapter 2 identifies environmental assets and green-economy opportunities within the BEIE diagnostic, while Chapter 3 identifies the Tragedy of the Commons and related resource-governance dynamics as systems risks. The appropriate policy response is therefore to strengthen property, access, monitoring, benefit-sharing and enforcement arrangements before scaling monetization mechanisms.

Implementation measure: Track policy issuance, implementing rules, community agreements, hectares under verified management/restoration, eligible PES or carbon projects, and realized revenues separately. Revenue estimates should be treated as scenario values until project-level feasibility and market validation are completed.

BSC/CLP link: LP5 → relevant Internal Process and Financial objectives in Chapters 5–6.

Recommendation 7.2: Strengthen the Bangsamoro Investment Facilitation Network (BIF-Net)

Primary actors: Chief Minister's Office; BOI-MTIT; participating BMOAs, LGUs and infrastructure/service agencies.

Action: Formalize BIF-Net as the cross-agency investment-facilitation network, with a defined mandate, service standards, project-escalation protocol, shared investment pipeline and digital tracking interface. BIF-Net should coordinate rather than duplicate the mandates of line ministries and existing investment institutions.

Rationale: Chapters 2, 3 and 6 consistently identify ecosystem synchronization as a central implementation requirement. BIF-Net provides the coordination layer through which project dependencies, investor requirements, permitting issues and cross-agency bottlenecks can be surfaced and resolved.

Implementation measure: Monitor investment inquiries, facilitated projects, permit/escalation cases, time-to-resolution, inter-agency service-level compliance and investment-pipeline conversion. Targets should be calibrated against an initial verified baseline.

BSC/CLP link: LP3 → Internal Process coordination → investment facilitation → Financial outcomes.

Recommendation 7.3: Accelerate the Bangsamoro Halal Industry Development Plan

Primary actors: MTIT and Bangsamoro halal institutions; Bangsamoro Parliament; relevant national standards and financial institutions.

Action: Strengthen halal certification capacity, laboratory and quality-assurance capability, technical training, mutual-recognition engagement and market-development support, with particular attention to MSMEs and producer groups.

Rationale: The BEIE diagnostic identifies halal capability and cultural legitimacy as important comparative assets. Chapter 3 incorporates halal-economy development into the strategic-option architecture, while Chapter 5 maps certification and enterprise capability into stakeholder and financial outcomes.

Implementation measure: Track certification applications, median processing time, certified MSMEs, laboratory utilization, mutual-recognition milestones, domestic sales and export transactions. The Chapter 4 headline pathway of 3,000 halal-certified MSMEs by 2030 and 5,000+ by 2035 should remain subject to baseline verification and annual calibration.

BSC/CLP link: LP1 → capability and certification → MSME participation → market and export outcomes.

Recommendation 7.4: Build an Interoperable Digital Governance and Investment Information Layer

Primary actors: Bangsamoro digital/governance institutions; BOI-MTIT; BIF-Net; participating BMOAs and LGUs.

Action: Establish interoperable digital workflows for investment registration, project monitoring, permits, investor requests, PAP status and KPI reporting. Apply privacy, security, access-control and data-governance standards consistent with applicable law.

Rationale: Chapters 4–6 require reliable, timely data for KPI calibration, BSC review and implementation learning. Digital systems should therefore be designed as management infrastructure, not simply as front-end service portals.

Implementation measure: Track service completion time, digital transaction share, data completeness, system availability, unresolved cases and cross-agency data-match rates.

BSC/CLP link: LP3 → data and process capability → service quality → investment outcomes.

B. Planning, Budgeting and Territorial Delivery

The second policy task is to translate the BEIE architecture into planning and budgeting decisions. Chapter 6 establishes three implementation phases—Phase 1: Foundation Building (2026–2028), Phase 2: Acceleration (2029–2031), and Phase 3: Consolidation and Global Integration (2032–2035). Policy and investment decisions should respect this dependency structure rather than treating the roadmap as an undifferentiated project list.

Recommendation 7.5: Apply Coherent Sequencing to Capital Budgeting

Primary actors: BPDA; MFBM; implementing BMOAs; provincial and municipal LGUs.

Action: Use BEIE dependencies, project readiness, economic linkages, safeguards, operating requirements and market access as explicit criteria in the MYIP, AIP and PAP review process. Infrastructure should be sequenced where it unlocks productive capacity, logistics, energy, digital access or market formation.

Rationale: Chapter 3's systems analysis shows that constraints in one part of the ecosystem can limit returns from investment elsewhere. Chapter 6 consequently places sequencing and dependency management at the center of implementation.

Implementation measure: Track readiness-at-approval, dependency completion, budget execution, time-to-completion, utilization and outcome realization. Avoid using project expenditure alone as a proxy for economic impact.

BSC/CLP link: LP2 → infrastructure and process capability → ecosystem throughput → financial and stakeholder outcomes.

Recommendation 7.6: Establish Province- and SGA-Specific Investment Delivery Packages

Primary actors: Provincial LGUs and the SGA; BOI-MTIT/BIF-Net; relevant technical BMOAs.

Action: Convert the Chapter 2 territorial diagnostics into investment-delivery packages that combine infrastructure, enterprise development, skills, finance, market access and safeguards. Packages should be tailored to verified provincial opportunities and constraints rather than applying identical interventions across territories.

Recommendation 7.7: Strengthen MSME Formalization, Digitalization and Resilience

Primary actors: MTIT-ETD; BIF-Net; MICT/digital institutions; financial institutions; TESDA and enterprise-support partners.

Action: Integrate Negosyo Center services, digital commerce, enterprise diagnostics, skills development, financing referral and halal/quality certification into a coordinated MSME support pathway.

Rationale: Chapter 2 identifies market-linkage, skills, technology adoption and financial-access constraints. Chapter 5's causal architecture connects learning and process capabilities to enterprise and stakeholder outcomes. A coordinated MSME pathway therefore addresses multiple constraints without requiring a separate program for every problem.

Implementation measure: Track formalization, digital adoption, financing referrals and approvals, certification, survival, sales, employment and women/youth participation. Use cohort-based monitoring where feasible so that support can be related to enterprise outcomes rather than only activity counts.

BSC/CLP link: LP1 + LP4 → capability → enterprise participation → income, employment and investment outcomes.

C. Investment Mobilization and Market Development

Policy recommendations for investors should focus on investable propositions supported by credible enabling conditions, rather than presenting speculative returns as established outcomes. Chapter 3 identifies four strategic options—Halal Economy Dominance/Development, Green Economy Monetization/Activation, Infrastructure-First Enabling, and Integrated Ecosystem Development—and Chapter 6 translates these into phased implementation. The following investment themes therefore represent areas for structured pipeline development and due diligence.

Recommendation 7.8: Build an Investable Halal Agro-Processing Pipeline

Target investors: Domestic food processors, ASEAN agribusiness, halal-oriented international firms, impact investors and development-finance institutions.

Action: Package bankable projects for halal-certified food, seaweed/carrageenan, coconut products, rubber processing, fisheries and other verified value-chain opportunities. Link projects to certification, utilities, logistics, supplier development and market/off-take arrangements.

Rationale: The halal pathway is a cross-cluster proposition: enterprise capability, certification, finance, logistics and market access must operate together. The investment case should therefore be evaluated at value-chain level rather than by processing plant alone.

Investor discipline: Require feasibility studies, feedstock assessments, utility confirmation, market/off-take evidence, ESG and safeguards review, capital structure, sensitivity analysis and exit/repayment assumptions before presenting project-level returns.

Recommendation 7.9: Develop Renewable Energy and Green Infrastructure Projects

Target investors: Independent power producers, infrastructure funds, climate-finance institutions, development partners and Shariah-compliant investors.

Action: Develop a project pipeline covering grid and distribution improvements, renewable generation, mini-grids, productive-use energy, biomass where feedstock is demonstrably sustainable, and energy systems serving industrial/agro-processing zones.

Rationale: Chapter 2 identifies infrastructure and energy constraints across the ecosystem, while Chapters 3 and 6 establish infrastructure-first sequencing and green-economy pathways. Projects should be prioritized where reliable energy unlocks productive activity and where technical and commercial feasibility is demonstrated.

Investor discipline: Validate resource assessments, grid interconnection, tariffs/off-take, land, permits, safeguards, currency and financing risks before assigning project-level IRRs or carbon-revenue assumptions.

Recommendation 7.10: Develop Sustainable Tourism and Cultural-Heritage Investment Platforms

Target investors: Hospitality operators, tourism enterprises, community-based organizations, cultural institutions and development-finance partners.

Action: Develop investable tourism corridors and destination packages that combine access infrastructure, hospitality, cultural interpretation, environmental safeguards, local enterprise participation and halal-friendly services where appropriate.

Rationale: The tourism opportunity identified in the roadmap depends on the interaction of cultural assets, connectivity, safety, services, environmental stewardship and local enterprise capability. Investment should therefore be destination-system based rather than limited to individual accommodation projects.

Investor discipline: Establish verified visitor baselines, carrying-capacity assessments, seasonality assumptions, community-benefit arrangements, environmental safeguards and market-demand evidence before setting revenue or return expectations.

Recommendation 7.11: Expand Islamic Finance and Climate-Resilient Financial Services

Target investors and partners: Islamic banks, fintech firms, takaful providers, microfinance institutions, development-finance institutions and institutional investors.

Action: Develop Shariah-compliant financing products for MSMEs, agriculture, climate adaptation, productive-use energy and eligible infrastructure; strengthen digital access; and assess appropriate sukuk or blended-finance structures for bankable projects.

Rationale: Chapter 2 identifies financial-access constraints, while Chapter 5 links financial intermediation to investment mobilization. The financing architecture should be designed around actual project cash flows, risk-sharing requirements and borrower capability rather than product availability alone.

Investor discipline: Validate credit demand, repayment capacity, portfolio risk, regulatory requirements, Shariah governance, guarantees and blended-finance assumptions before setting portfolio-level return expectations.

D. Governance of the Way Forward

1. Maintain the Three-Phase Investment Logic

Policy decisions should preserve the sequencing established in Chapter 6: 2026–2028 for foundation building; 2029–2031 for acceleration; and 2032–2035 for consolidation and global integration. Projects may advance at different speeds, but dependencies should be explicit and deviations documented.

2. Use BIF-Net as the Coordination Layer

BIF-Net should maintain a shared investment and PAP pipeline, coordinate inter-agency issue resolution, surface dependencies, and support investor aftercare. It should not replace statutory mandates of BMOAs, LGUs, regulators or financial institutions. Its value is in reducing coordination friction and making the ecosystem legible to decision-makers and investors.

3. Govern Targets Through Evidence and Change Control

Chapter 4 establishes a calibration discipline: verify baselines, assign target ownership, document target changes, and classify variance into data-quality, implementation, or external-shock variance. Chapter 7 should reinforce this rule rather than introduce new unsupported targets. Where administrative data are unavailable or not yet verified, the roadmap should state the gap and define the process for closing it.

4. Review the Roadmap as a Learning System

The BIRD architecture is intended to be adaptive. Annual reviews should test whether assumptions remain valid, whether dependencies are being resolved, whether investments are producing intended intermediate outcomes, and whether external conditions require sequencing changes. Changes should be traceable to evidence and approved through the appropriate governance process.

The Decisive Decade

The 2026–2035 horizon provides a common planning frame within which policy, investment, institutional capacity and enterprise development can be synchronized. The decisive issue is not the number of initiatives launched, but whether the underlying system becomes progressively more capable of converting investment into durable economic, social and environmental value.

The preceding chapters establish the architecture for that transition. Chapter 2 shows how the BEIE clusters and systems dynamics interact. Chapter 3 translates the diagnostic into strategic options, leverage points and phased sequencing. Chapter 4 establishes KPI calibration and measurement discipline. Chapter 5 links objectives through a Balanced Scorecard and causal architecture. Chapter 6 converts that architecture into governance, PAPs, annual actions and MEL. Chapter 7 completes the chain by identifying the policy, institutional and investment decisions required to sustain execution.

The roadmap is strongest when policy, planning, investment, implementation and learning operate as one system: evidence informs priorities; priorities shape investment; investment produces outcomes; and outcomes improve the next cycle of decisions.

A Shared Responsibility

The realization of BIRD 2026–2035 requires coordinated responsibility. The Bangsamoro Parliament establishes enabling policy and appropriations within its mandate. The Chief Minister and executive leadership provide direction and cross-government coordination. BMOAs and LGUs translate strategy into implementable PAPs and services. BIF-Net facilitates cross-agency investment coordination and investor service. Financial institutions and investors mobilize capital subject to commercial, regulatory, environmental and social due diligence. Development partners can provide technical assistance, risk-sharing and co-financing. Civil society, communities and enterprises contribute local knowledge, participation, accountability and market activity.

The Way Forward

The next step is disciplined execution. Policy commitments should be translated into accountable actions; actions should be linked to funded and implementation-ready PAPs; PAPs should be monitored through the KPI and MEL architecture; and results should feed back into the next investment and policy cycle. This is the central systems principle of BIRD 2026–2035: coordination creates coherence, evidence creates learning, and disciplined sequencing creates the conditions for durable investment-led development.

The Emerging Bangsamoro is not a destination.
It is a journey that begins with coordinated action.
