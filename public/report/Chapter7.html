Policy Recommendations and the Way Forward

Chapter summary

Chapters 1–6 establish the evidence base, BEIE diagnosis, Integrated Ecosystem Development Strategy (IEDS), performance measures, causal pathways, and phased implementation architecture. Chapter 7 converts those findings into a coordinated policy and institutional agenda. It addresses the conditions needed to sustain delivery through political transitions, fiscal constraints, climate shocks and security disruptions.

The central policy proposition is that investment opportunities and incentives will not by themselves produce transformation. The binding constraint identified in the preceding chapters is institutional throughput: the ability to turn a credible opportunity into a prepared project, lawful decision, financed investment, operating enterprise and measurable public value. Recommendations therefore prioritise institutional clarity, investability, reliable data and accountable delivery—not an undifferentiated expansion of incentives.

Why this chapter exists

A roadmap fails in one of three ways. It is ignored when governments change; it is executed in silos so that one cluster's success is cancelled by another's bottleneck; or it drifts, with targets quietly lowered to match performance. Chapter 2 named these patterns as the Fixes that Fail, Shifting the Burden and Drifting Goals archetypes (Senge, 1990), and Chapter 3 located the leverage points that counter them (Meadows, 1999). Stakeholders confirmed the risk: drifting goals scored a vulnerability index of 3.03 of 5, and political transition 2.85 of 5, in the Validation Survey. Chapter 7 therefore treats continuity as a design problem rather than an aspiration.

The chapter is organised around the four areas set out in the Executive Summary: (A) policy and institutional enablers; (B) planning, budgeting and territorial delivery; (C) investment mobilisation and market development; and (D) governance of the way forward.

Relationship among the four instruments. BIRD supplies strategic direction and results logic; the Investment Code should supply statutory authority and rules on registration/incentives; the BIF-Net EO and implementing rules should organise case coordination and inter-agency facilitation within existing legal mandates; and the Investors’ Guidebook should translate approved rules and verified procedures into an accessible investor journey. The Guidebook does not create rights or incentives, the EO cannot amend statutes or waive statutory requirements, and the Roadmap does not itself appropriate funds or approve projects. All four should use a common, version-controlled evidence and performance framework.

A. Policy and Institutional Enablers

The policy stack contains instruments with different legal status: the Investment Code is a working legislative draft; the BIF-Net Executive Order is proposed; the Investors’ Guidebook is an informational and procedural resource; and the 2026 Strategic Investment Priority Plan is a national instrument cited in the evidence base. Their status must be verified with the relevant authorities before any issuance, enactment or public promise. The aim of Section A is to create one coherent investment system without treating a draft, an administrative instrument, a national plan and an investor guide as legally equivalent.

A.2 Resolve council overlap and harmonise the institutional model

The draft Investment Code proposes a Bangsamoro Investment Development Council (BIDC), the proposed BIF-Net Executive Order establishes a BIF-Net Council, and Chapter 6 describes a Bangsamoro Investment Command Center (BICC) as an implementation and performance-management hub. Their functions and reporting relationships overlap in the current drafts, creating a risk of duplicate approvals, unclear accountability and competing data systems. The evidence from Chapters 2–3 on institutional bottlenecks and escalation supports resolving this overlap before operationalisation. The final design must be settled through legal review against the Bangsamoro Organic Law, Administrative Code and other applicable laws; an Executive Order must not displace powers that require legislation or are assigned by statute.

A2
Resolve institutional overlap and adopt one coordinated operating model. Subject to legal review, use one policy council for cross-government investment coordination, one BIF-Net network for inter-agency facilitation, and one designated Secretariat/operating office within MTIT–BOI for case management, data and aftercare. Determine whether BIDC and the BIF-Net Council should be merged, reconstituted or assigned distinct non-duplicative functions in the Code and EO. Define the BICC's status as a function or operating unit—not an additional approval body—unless a separate legal basis is established. Preserve BBOI, BEZA, ministry, national-agency and LGU statutory decision rights. Add private-sector and LGU participation with conflict-of-interest, confidentiality and recusal rules. Owner: OCM and MTIT, with legislative counsel and relevant agencies. Horizon: reconcile in the amended EO and Code package before final issuance/enactment.

A.3 BIF-Net as ecosystem orchestrator

BIF-Net should act as the facilitation and orchestration layer for the IEDS, not as a new licensing or incentive-granting authority. Consistent with the proposed EO, its core functions are to provide a “No Wrong Door” entry point, maintain a central case registry, assign a lead investment officer, coordinate referrals and parallel processing where lawful, monitor agreed service standards, escalate unresolved inter-agency bottlenecks, and provide investor aftercare. Chapters 5–6 require the same operating system to connect the investment pipeline with the Balanced Scorecard (BSC), Programs–Activities–Projects (PAPs), budgets and MEL. Any cluster councils or coordination forums should be subordinate coordination mechanisms, not additional statutory approval gates.

A.4 Standards, finance and energy institutions

Halal assurance (LP1). International buyers do not buy a label; they buy the credibility of the body behind it. Halal certification weakness is rated a 15.65 of 25 risk and standards recognition a 2.77 of 5 threat. The policy priority is institutional: a certification authority (the Bangsamoro Halal Board referred to in Chapter 6) with a statutory basis, an accreditation path aligned with OIC/SMIIC requirements for halal certification bodies, laboratory capacity in Cotabato City and Marawi, and a mutual-recognition sequence that starts with BIMP-EAGA partners and extends to GCC markets as accreditation matures.

A4
Put halal assurance on a statutory and accredited footing. Owner: halal certification authority with MTIT and MOST. Horizon: statutory basis and accreditation plan in Phase 1; first mutual-recognition arrangement in Phase 2; SMIIC-aligned recognition by 2035.

Islamic finance (LP4). Banking regulation is a national function: the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas licenses and supervises Islamic banks under RA 11439. The Bangsamoro role is to build demand, pipeline, Shariah governance capacity and public-sector use of Islamic instruments. Among respondents, 72.3% consider a coordinating Islamic finance authority essential and a further 18.5% support one once basic banking is strengthened. Their top actions are financial literacy (29.4%), human capital (23.5%) and regulatory clarity, including tax neutrality (22.1%).

A5
Establish a Bangsamoro Islamic finance coordination council. Located in MFBM, with Bangsamoro Darul-Ifta advisory and a working protocol with BSP. Mandate: literacy and talent programmes, a sukuk-ready project pipeline, takaful for climate and agricultural risk, and advocacy on national tax-neutrality rules. It does not license or supervise institutions. Owner: MFBM. Horizon: Phase 1.

Energy (LP2). The DOE and MENRE signed a transition agreement in 2026 to devolve power-sector regulation and development functions to MENRE in phases. Devolution creates an opportunity and a capacity risk. Reported electrification also depends heavily on definition: about 40% of potential household connections in BARMM electric cooperative areas (NEA, 2023), against 67.52% of households region-wide at end-2025 in the devolution announcement. Transmission links such as the Zamboanga–Basilan interconnection fall under the grid operator's franchise and ERC regulation, so they require a national interface rather than regional procurement alone.

A6
Make energy devolution investable. Adopt one household-electrification metric for all BIRD reporting; build MENRE's regulatory and planning capacity on a published schedule; prioritise electric cooperative turnaround and island mini-grids; and coordinate transmission projects with NGCP, ERC and DOE through the national interface. Owner: MENRE. Horizon: Phase 1, reviewed annually.
Why these are institutional recommendations. Each of A4–A6 strengthens a body that must exist and perform before incentives can work. Granting incentives to halal exporters without a recognised certifier, or to agro-processors without reliable power, would reproduce the Fixes that Fail pattern documented in Chapter 2.

B.2 Budgeting for delivery, not intention

A plan's value is the portion of it that becomes output. Underspending is one of the highest-rated weaknesses in the survey (16.85 of 25), and infrastructure cost overruns are among the highest-rated threats (3.07 of 5). Two budgeting reforms address both. First, tag every budget line that funds a BIRD programme to its BEIE cluster and leverage point, so that the Parliament can see the investment logic in the appropriations. Second, align later-phase releases with verified project readiness, procurement and delivery gates, as Chapter 6 proposes. Any central contingency reserve should be separately justified, costed and authorised through the budget process.

The first-year action plan illustrates concentration risk: the chapter’s illustrative budget table assigns ₱2.0 billion of ₱2.063 billion to one transmission project, while the remaining institutional actions are much smaller. These are planning figures that should be reconciled to approved appropriations and current project estimates before execution. Protect critical institutional and project-preparation activities from routine reallocation through lawful budget tagging, readiness gates and transparent variance reporting; do not imply that this chapter can ring-fence funds outside the appropriations process.

B2
Link expenditure to readiness, delivery and results. Tag BIRD-related budget items by PAP, BEIE cluster and leverage point; connect release decisions to approved appropriations, project readiness, procurement milestones and verified outputs; and report quarterly on obligations, disbursements, physical progress, cost variance and delays. Protect essential institutional and project-preparation work through the formal budget process. Treat the BSC’s >90% execution target as a management target, not a substitute for output verification: high expenditure without output should trigger review. Any 20% contingency reserve or other funding rule must be separately costed, authorised and reflected in the budget. Owner: MFBM with BPDA and implementing agencies. Horizon: 2027 budget cycle onward.
B4
Sequence Enablers by the Foundations pipeline they unlock. Prioritise farm-to-market roads, cold chain, port upgrades and power for named agro-processing and fisheries projects, rather than infrastructure in the abstract. Stakeholders favour physical connectivity (58.9%) and market-access assets (32.9%) over digital backbones (8.2%) as the first priority. Owner: MPW and MAFAR through the Enablers council. Horizon: Phase 1 project list by Q3 2027.
B5
Close the skills gap with an industry compact. Functional literacy (18.19 of 25) and skills mismatch (16.96) are the top-rated human-capital weaknesses. MBHTE's technical-education arm, MOLE and cluster councils agree occupational skills plans tied to the investment pipeline, with employer co-design and placement targets. Owner: MBHTE and MOLE. Horizon: first plans in 2027; annual update.
A note on the target. Holding ₱550B as the 2035 GRDP goal is a strategic ambition, but the implied annual growth rate depends on whether the target and base use comparable current-price or constant-price series, geography and rebasing. The current illustrative calculation uses the cited ₱266.59B five-province 2025 base and produces approximately 7.5% annual growth under the stated assumptions; it should not be treated as a verified real-growth requirement until the price basis and official series are confirmed. The register (B1) should publish the reproducible calculation and annual path so that slippage is visible early.

B.3 Territorial delivery across the Bangsamoro

One model will not serve a mainland agro-industrial basin, a lake-and-highland province, a freshly organised Special Geographic Area, an administrative city and two island provinces. The territorial units for BIRD are the five provinces of Lanao del Sur, Maguindanao del Norte, Maguindanao del Sur, Basilan (excluding Isabela City) and Tawi-Tawi, together with the Special Geographic Area and Cotabato City. Two boundary facts matter for delivery. Basilan's provincial seat, Isabela City, lies outside BARMM, so BARMM-side delivery in Basilan should run through Lamitan and municipal centres, with a cross-boundary arrangement for port and logistics links through Isabela City and Zamboanga City. Sulu is now outside the region; it is a neighbour and corridor partner, not a delivery territory.

B3
Implement a transparent, place-based delivery approach. Use provincial diagnostics and documented project readiness to allocate technical assistance, project preparation and enabling infrastructure. Prioritise appropriate delivery nodes for Bongao, Lamitan and the SGA, and establish lawful cross-boundary arrangements with Region IX for Basilan’s logistics links, noting that Isabela City is outside BARMM and Sulu is a corridor partner rather than a delivery territory. C. Investment Mobilisation and Market Development

C.1 From incentives to investability

Incentives alone do not establish investability. A project also needs lawful registration and eligibility, secure site and tenure arrangements, predictable permitting, available infrastructure and power, credible market/offtake, appropriate safeguards, and risks that can be priced. The Code must align any regional incentive provisions with applicable national law and the jurisdiction of the relevant investment promotion agency; the Guidebook must explain actual eligibility rather than advertise incentives as automatic. BARMM's comparative advantages are its halal credibility, its Islamic finance potential and its BIMP-EAGA position. Investment mobilisation should convert these into a pipeline of prepared projects.

C1
Establish a project-preparation function and quality-controlled pipeline. Prepare priority projects through pre-feasibility, land/tenure and site due diligence, environmental and social safeguards, climate and conflict sensitivity, market/offtake testing, cost estimates and transaction advice. For every entry, record the sponsor, location, stage, dependencies, approvals required, capital need, financing status, risks and next decision. Use clear statuses—concept, under preparation, feasibility-complete, approval-ready, financing-committed, under construction and operating. Do not describe an opportunity as bankable or investment-ready until the evidence and responsible sponsor support that designation. Owner: MTIT–BOI with BPDA, sector agencies, LGUs and development partners. Horizon: facility design by Q4 2027 and an initial prepared-project cohort in 2028, subject to funding and readiness.

C.2 Market corridors and standards

Asked where to point the export effort, 52.1% of respondents chose BIMP-EAGA and UAE/GCC markets together, 35.6% BIMP-EAGA first and 12.3% UAE/GCC first. The two corridors have different entry conditions. BIMP-EAGA markets are near, culturally close and reachable with existing products; GCC markets pay more but require recognised standards and reliable logistics. The policy implication is a sequence within a parallel strategy: build volume and traceability in BIMP-EAGA now, while the standards work in A4 opens GCC access.

C2
Run a two-corridor market strategy. Prioritise BIMP-EAGA trade facilitation, Tawi-Tawi and Polloc logistics and buyer-supplier matching in Phase 1; develop GCC access through accredited halal certification and traceability as recognition is secured. Owner: MTIT with BEZA and the Connectors council. Horizon: Phase 1 for BIMP-EAGA; Phase 2 for GCC scale-up.

C.3 The capital stack

The Roadmap models a ₱120–160 billion investment envelope and an indicative 11–14× leverage requirement. These figures should be treated as planning assumptions until a costed financing plan and commitments are verified; they are not evidence that funds have been appropriated or that private capital is secured. The Validation Survey indicates stakeholder support for blended finance (87.5%), with preferences for infrastructure-scale instruments (56.3%), MSME access (25.0%) and risk mitigation (18.8%). These are preferences, not proof of bankability. The financing architecture should combine project preparation, clearly allocated public and private risks, development-finance guarantees where available, Shariah-compatible financing, MSME capital and climate/agricultural risk coverage, subject to mandates and market testing.

C3
Develop an auditable, blended and Shariah-compatible financing plan. For each priority programme/project, disclose the capital source, instrument, expected timing, financing gap, risk allocation, readiness status and responsible sponsor. Report public appropriations, signed commitments, indicative interest and aspirational mobilisation separately; define how the 11–14× leverage assumption is calculated and avoid counting the same capital twice. Establish first-loss, guarantee, sukuk, MSME-finance or takaful proposals only where a qualified financier and lawful operating arrangement are identified. Owner: MFBM with MTIT–BOI, project sponsors and prospective financing partners. Horizon: financing plan in Phase 1, updated quarterly as commitments change.

C.4 Aftercare, retention and reinvestment

The cheapest investment to attract is the next one from a firm already operating in the region. Reinvestment depends on how existing investors are treated: whether problems are solved, whether rules are stable and whether the government listens. The Balanced Scorecard target of investor satisfaction at 8.0 of 10 by 2035 has no baseline yet, and the investor information that exists is spread across separate documents and systems.

C4
Institutionalise investor aftercare and grievance resolution. Assign a case/account officer for facilitated and registered investors according to risk and service capacity; maintain a confidential channel for complaints and a de-identified systemic-issue log; track response, resolution and recurrence; and record actual versus pledged investment, employment and reinvestment using the proper agency data owner. Establish an investor-satisfaction baseline before claiming progress against the Chapter 5 target of 8.0/10 by 2035. Owner: designated BIF-Net Secretariat with BBOI, BEZA and relevant agencies. Horizon: phased start from 2027, with annual reporting.
C5
Rebuild the Investors’ Guidebook as a controlled, investor-facing service product. Publish a version date, content owner, change log and correction channel. For each investor pathway, state the responsible office, legal basis, eligibility, documents, fees where applicable, official processing time, sequence, referral route and escalation channel. Clearly distinguish statutory processing periods from BIF-Net service standards; explain Green Lane eligibility and the fact that facilitation does not waive legal requirements. Use the canonical baseline register (B1) and label opportunities as concepts, under preparation, finance-ready, approved, under construction or operational—never describe an unverified concept as an approved or bankable project. Owner: MTIT–BOI with BBOI, BEZA and member agencies. Horizon: first controlled digital edition in 2027 and updates when rules or agency procedures change, with a full annual review.

D. Governance of the Way Forward

D.1 Transition-proofing the roadmap

The first regular Bangsamoro Parliament was elected on 14 September 2026. No party won a majority: the Bangsamoro Federalist Party and the United Bangsamoro Justice Party lead with close seat counts, so the Chief Minister will be elected through coalition (Reuters, 2026). A ten-year roadmap that belongs to one administration will not survive its successor. BIRD's continuity therefore depends on being adopted as a shared framework rather than a partisan programme.

D1
Adopt BIRD as a shared, cross-party investment framework. Present BIRD to the first session for endorsement by resolution; feed it into the next Bangsamoro Development Plan as the investment chapter; and require any change to headline targets to pass through the baseline register and the agreed governance body with a published rationale. Owner: OCM, MTIT and BPDA. Horizon: endorsement within the first parliamentary year.

D.2 Learning, evidence and review

Respondents prefer quarterly adaptive review (51.4%) over annual review (31.4%). The current evidence base also has known limits. About three-quarters of respondents are government-affiliated, only 11 of 76 (14.5%) are from the private sector, and Basilan and Tawi-Tawi returned none. Cotabato City (27) and Maguindanao del Norte (22) supply most responses. Findings about the island provinces are therefore judgements made about them, not by them.

D2
Run a second validation wave with quotas. In 2027, repeat validation with minimum quotas for Basilan, Tawi-Tawi, the SGA and the private sector, using province-based workshops where online access is weak. Remove response options that refer to territory outside BARMM. Owner: MTIT-BoI with BPDA. Horizon: before the first annual BSC assessment.
D3
Apply one evidence and reporting contract across the Roadmap, BIF-Net and investor information. Keep (1) official statistics and administrative records, (2) stakeholder-validation survey results, and (3) management targets, forecasts and financing assumptions visibly separate. Every published indicator should show source, date, geography, definition and status; survey measures should be labelled as perceptions and not administrative baselines. Link BIF-Net case data and Guidebook content to the controlled register where appropriate, while protecting confidential business information and personal data. Report the conversion chain separately: inquiry → qualified pipeline → registration/approval → permits/facilitation → realised investment → jobs, exports and wider outcomes. Owner: BPDA and MTIT–BOI with official data owners. Horizon: immediate design rule; continuous application.

Closing statement

RulesInstitutionsInformationDelivery capacityInvestable projectsCapitalJobs and resilience

BIRD 2026–2035 does not ask BARMM to rely on a single sector, one incentive or one administrative body. It recommends a coherent sequence: use the evidence in Chapters 1–2 to identify constraints; apply the IEDS and leverage points in Chapter 3; maintain the calibrated KPIs and baseline discipline in Chapter 4; manage the causal pathways and Balanced Scorecard in Chapter 5; and implement the budgeted PAPs and MEL cycle in Chapter 6. The Investment Code should provide lawful and durable authority; the BIF-Net EO and rules should organise lawful inter-agency facilitation; the Investors’ Guidebook should explain verified requirements and opportunities; and the Roadmap should align all three with territorial priorities, financing and measurable results. This architecture will build investor confidence only if responsibilities are clear, commitments are funded, legal safeguards are respected and performance is reported against verifiable evidence.
