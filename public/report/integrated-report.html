    Bangsamoro Investment Roadmap Development 2026–2035

    Revealing the Bangsamoro's hidden treasure: an integrated, ecosystem-based investment strategy for a connected region.

    Ministry of Trade, Investments and Tourism – Bureau of Investments (MTIT-BOI)
    With BPDA, MAFAR, MENRE, MPW, BBOI, BEZA and provincial LGUs
    Cotabato City, Bangsamoro. Integrated edition, October 2026.
    Scope: Lanao del Sur, Maguindanao del Norte, Maguindanao del Sur, Basilan (excluding Isabela City), Tawi-Tawi, Cotabato City and the SGA.

    Executive Summary

    Pathways to sustainable economic resilience for the Bangsamoro, 2026–2035.

    This report sets out how the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao can build sustainable economic resilience between 2026 and 2035. It combines financial discipline, inclusive governance and socio-political foresight to move the region beyond resource dependency toward a connected, innovation-driven economy. Its ambition is that BARMM becomes Southeast Asia's premier halal, agro-industrial and sustainable investment destination by 2035 — an ambition this report treats as a direction to be earned niche by niche, not a claim to be asserted.

    The roadmap covers BARMM as it now stands: Lanao del Sur, Maguindanao del Norte, Maguindanao del Sur, Basilan (excluding Isabela City), Tawi-Tawi, Cotabato City and the Special Geographic Area. Sulu and Isabela City are outside its scope, so that every strategy remains specific to territory the Bangsamoro Government governs.

    Where the region stands

    BARMM's reported GRDP reached ₱299.5 billion in 2024, growing 2.7 percent — slower than the preceding year, with Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing contracting 4.2 percent after El Niño and rubber disease. Services account for 42.0 percent of output, AFF for 32.4 percent and Industry for 25.6 percent. Investment approvals passed ₱5.1 billion in the first months of 2026. Poverty, at 34.8 percent in the first half of 2023, remains the highest in the country, and functional literacy, at 59.3 percent, the lowest.

    What has changed in 2026

    Two events reset the planning baseline. The Supreme Court's ruling on Sulu reduced and fragmented the territory, leaving Basilan and Tawi-Tawi as non-contiguous island provinces. And on 14 September 2026 the region elected its first Parliament, which takes office on 30 October. The roadmap plans against a reference scenario of a functioning but contested transition, and sets out signposts for revising that view.

    Strategic pillars

    • Halal economy — build a certification and production system recognised under OIC/SMIIC standards, targeting a share of the global halal market estimated at USD 2.3 trillion.
    • Agro-industrial transformation — process rubber, coconut, seaweed, cassava and fisheries inside the region instead of exporting them raw.
    • Green economy — make forests and marine ecosystems pay for their own protection through carbon credits, Payment for Ecosystem Services and eco-tourism fees, under the pending Bangsamoro Forestry Code.
    • Connectivity and infrastructure — close gaps in power, ports, roads and broadband that keep the island provinces apart from the mainland core.
    • Islamic finance — expand Shariah-compliant banking, takaful and microfinance so capital reaches MSMEs and farmers.
    • Human capital — treat literacy and skills as the binding constraint on every other pillar.

    How the roadmap is delivered

    Implementation runs in three phases: Foundation Building (2026–2028), Acceleration (2029–2032), and Consolidation and Global Integration (2033–2035). Progress is tracked through OIC/SMIIC, ESG and Philippine Development Plan benchmarks and a Balanced Scorecard. Three institutions with similar names carry distinct roles: the Bangsamoro Board of Investments (BBOI) registers investments and grants regional incentives; the MTIT–Bureau of Investments (MTIT-BOI) promotes investment, runs the one-stop action center and serves as secretariat to inter-agency coordination; and the national Board of Investments (BOI) under the Department of Trade and Industry administers national incentives such as those under the CREATE MORE Act.

    Principal risks

    The targets in this roadmap depend on assumptions that may not hold: a stable first parliamentary term; timely enactment of the Investment and Forestry Codes; conversion of investment approvals into construction (approvals are lumpy and are not realised investment); re-based statistics after the Sulu ruling; and climate shocks to an economy still one-third agricultural. Each chapter states its own assumptions and data sources.

    Chapter 1: Introduction: Vision, Mandate and Scope

    Why this roadmap, why now, and the territory it plans for.

    1.1 Background and Rationale

    The Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) was formally established on February 26, 2019, following the ratification of the Bangsamoro Organic Law (Republic Act No. 11054) and the historic plebiscite that affirmed the region's autonomous status. The Bangsamoro Autonomous Region represents not merely an administrative reorganization but a transformative political settlement — one that seeks to convert decades of armed conflict into a durable framework for self-governance, cultural affirmation, and economic self-determination.

    Six years into this governance experiment, the data confirms that the peace dividend is real. BARMM's poverty incidence has fallen from 55.9 percent in 2018 to 34.8 percent in the first semester of 2023 — still the highest in the Philippines but a remarkable improvement. Investment approvals surged from negligible levels before 2019 to ₱5.1 billion in the first four months of 2026 alone. The region has moved from a narrative of conflict to one of cautious but genuine investor interest.

    The Bangsamoro Investment Roadmap Development (BIRD) 2026–2035 is the BARMM Ministry of Trade, Investments and Tourism's strategic response to this moment. It is a ten-year blueprint for accelerating investment-led growth — one that is evidence-based, ecosystem-oriented, and anchored in the region's unique comparative advantages: its halal identity, agricultural endowments, strategic location within BIMP-EAGA, and a young, growing population of 5.69 million.

    1.2 Scope and Coverage

    This roadmap covers the territory of BARMM as it stands after the Supreme Court ruling on Sulu: the provinces of Lanao del Sur, Maguindanao del Norte, Maguindanao del Sur, Basilan (excluding Isabela City) and Tawi-Tawi; Cotabato City; and the Special Geographic Area (SGA) in Cotabato Province. The Province of Sulu and Isabela City are outside the scope of this roadmap. It provides in-depth economic profiles for Lanao del Sur and Basilan — the two provinces with the most recent comprehensive investment outlook analyses — and sector-specific strategies applicable across the region.

    Six priority investment sectors are addressed in depth: (1) Agro-Industry and Halal Food Value Chains; (2) Tourism and Cultural Economy; (3) Energy and Utilities; (4) Infrastructure, Logistics, and Connectivity; (5) Services (Health, Education, Financial, Digital); and (6) the emerging Green Economy and Environmental Markets cluster.

    Why the scope is drawn this way. In 2024 the Supreme Court ruled that the Province of Sulu, which rejected the Bangsamoro Organic Law in the 2019 plebiscite, is not part of BARMM, and Congress amended the Organic Law accordingly through Republic Act No. 12317 (2026). Isabela City in Basilan likewise voted against inclusion in 2019 and remains outside BARMM's jurisdiction. Strategies in this roadmap therefore cover only territory the Bangsamoro Government can legislate, budget and facilitate investment for. Partnerships with Sulu and Isabela City are treated as external, inter-regional relationships — important for trade corridors, but not for planning targets.

    Data note. Most regional indicators in this report (GRDP, population, poverty) are PSA series published before the Sulu ruling and therefore still include Sulu. Re-based series had not been published at the time of writing. Regional totals should be read as upper bounds for the current territory; provincial figures for the in-scope provinces are unaffected.

    1.3 Vision Statement

    By 2035, the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region is recognised as Southeast Asia's premier halal, agro-industrial and sustainable investment destination — a connected, self-sustaining, export-linked economy that delivers inclusive prosperity anchored in Islamic values, cultural identity and moral governance.

    1.4 Mission

    To develop and implement an integrated, ecosystem-based investment roadmap that attracts high-quality domestic and foreign investments, creates sustainable employment, reduces poverty, and advances the Bangsamoro's economic sovereignty through strategic facilitation, enabling governance, and multi-stakeholder collaboration.

    1.5 Guiding Principles

    • Moral Governance: Transparency, accountability, and Islamic ethics as the operating system of investment facilitation.

    • Ecosystem Thinking: Treating sectors as interconnected clusters rather than isolated silos.

    • Cultural Authenticity: Leveraging Bangsamoro identity — halal, Islamic finance, Maranao and Yakan heritage — as genuine competitive advantages.

    • Environmental Stewardship: Integrating green economy pathways, carbon markets, and nature-based solutions as economic opportunities.

    • Inclusivity: Ensuring that investment benefits reach MSMEs, farming communities, fisherfolk, women, and internally displaced persons.

    • Data-Driven Decision Making: Grounding all strategies in verified economic data and stakeholder consultation outputs.

    1.6 Regulatory and Policy Anchors

    The BIRD 2026–2035 operates within the following key legal and policy frameworks:

    • Bangsamoro Organic Law (Republic Act No. 11054, 2018)

    • BARMM 2nd Bangsamoro Development Plan (BDP) 2023–2028

    • BARMM 2022 Strategic Investment Priorities Plan (SIPP)

    • Bangsamoro Halal Industry Development Plan (BHIDP) 2025–2030

    • Bangsamoro Sustainable Energy Master Plan (BSEMP)

    • Republic Act No. 11439 (Islamic Banking Law)

    • CREATE MORE Act (RA 12066) — National tax incentive framework

    • Bangsamoro Forestry Code (currently before ENRE Committee, MP Tawakkal Midtimbang)

    • Draft MENRE-MILG Joint Memorandum Circular (JMC) No. 2026-01 — Framework for Carbon Credits, PES, and Eco-tourism User Fees

    • BIMP-EAGA EGL (Environment, Green Economy, and Land Use) Sub-Committee Strategic Plan 2025–2028

    • Philippine Development Plan (PDP) 2023–2028

    Chapter 2: The Operating Environment: PEDSET Scan and Scenarios

    The macro forces shaping investment decisions, and three futures to plan against.

    The Political, Economic, Demographics, Social, Environmental, and Technological (PEDSET) framework provides a comprehensive macro-environmental analysis of factors influencing investment decisions in BARMM.

    2.1 Political Domain

    BARMM's governance landscape is defined by a unique transition from a revolutionary movement to an administrative authority. The Bangsamoro Transition Authority (BTA), composed primarily of nominees of the Moro Islamic Liberation Front (MILF), has been governing the region since 2019. After four postponements, the first Bangsamoro parliamentary elections were held on 14 September 2026 under Republic Act No. 12317, which amended the Bangsamoro Organic Law following the Supreme Court ruling on Sulu. The elected Parliament takes office on 30 October 2026 for a term ending 30 June 2031, after which regional elections are synchronised with the national cycle. The handover from an appointed to an elected government is both the region's largest opportunity and its largest near-term source of policy uncertainty for investors.

    Key political developments shaping the investment climate include: the continued implementation of the Comprehensive Agreement on the Bangsamoro (CAB); the operationalization of the BARMM 2022 Strategic Investment Priorities Plan (SIPP); and the enactment of the Bangsamoro Investment Code (BIC) providing the legal architecture for investment incentives.

    The political stability dividend is evident in investment data: BARMM ranked second in Mindanao in approved investments in Q2 2025 (behind only the Davao Region) and placed in the top 10 regions nationally — a structural shift in investor perception from conflict risk to governance credibility.

    2.2 Economic Domain

    BARMM's economy demonstrated remarkable growth momentum from 2020 to 2023, with a compounded annual growth rate (CAGR) of approximately 6.14 percent. The GRDP reached ₱299.5 billion in 2024 (₱291.6 billion in 2023), growing at 2.7 percent in 2023–2024 — a deceleration from the 4.0 percent recorded in 2022–2023 but reflecting global macroeconomic headwinds rather than structural weakness.

    2.3 Demographics Domain

    BARMM's total population reached 5,691,583 as of July 1, 2024 — representing 5.0 percent of the Philippine population and growing at 3.43 percent annually during 2020–2024, nearly double the national average. This is simultaneously one of BARMM's greatest assets and most pressing challenges.

    The population is extremely youthful: the median age is significantly below the national average, translating into a growing labor force and an expanding domestic consumer market. However, structural unemployment, skills mismatches, and out-migration of professionals continue to constrain labor supply quality. Lanao del Sur holds the largest provincial population at 1.368 million (2024), while Basilan is the fastest-growing province with a 5.93 percent average annual population growth rate.

    2.4 Social Domain

    Social development indicators present a mixed picture. BARMM's poverty incidence of 34.8 percent in H1 2023 remains the highest nationally — more than double the 16.4 percent national rate. The Bangsamoro's functional literacy rate stands at just 59.3 percent (PSA FLEMMS 2024), the lowest in the Philippines. Child stunting affects 45 percent of children under five — also the highest nationally.

    The region's cultural and religious identity — rooted in Islam and expressed through the traditions of Maranao, Tausug, Yakan, Maguindanaon, and other ethno-linguistic groups — is a strength for the halal economy and cultural tourism, but also poses implementation challenges around culturally sensitive investment facilitation. Internal displacement remains a structural challenge: approximately 120,000 individuals in Lanao del Sur alone remained displaced as of early 2023.

    2.5 Environmental Domain

    BARMM's natural endowments are extraordinary: extensive agricultural lands in the Cotabato Basin; Lake Lanao, one of Southeast Asia's largest and deepest lakes; the Agus hydropower cascade supplying a significant share of Mindanao's electricity; coastal and marine ecosystems in Basilan and Tawi-Tawi; and forest resources spanning highland Lanao del Sur and the island of Basilan.

    The BARMM Sustainable Energy Master Plan (BSEMP) targets a renewable energy mix of 75.86 percent by 2028. Climate vulnerability is a significant risk: El Niño-induced drought in 2024 caused ₱346 million in crop losses in Maguindanao del Sur, and AFF sector growth contracted by 4.2 percent region-wide in 2024. The BIMP-EAGA EGL Sub-Committee Strategic Plan 2025–2028 frames BARMM's environmental assets as regional economic drivers — from carbon markets to eco-tourism to sustainable timber.

    Critically, the Bangsamoro Forestry Code — currently before the ENRE Committee chaired by MP Tawakkal Midtimbang — provides the legislative foundation for sustainable resource utilization, value-added forest products processing, and access to emerging environmental markets including REDD+ and Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES). When enacted, this code will be transformative for BARMM's green economy investment landscape.

    2.6 Technological Domain

    Digital transformation is both a challenge and an opportunity for BARMM. The Bangsamoro e-Governance Master Plan (BEGMP) provides a framework for digital public services, while the BARMM Broadband Plan targets last-mile connectivity. However, information and communication technology (ICT) gaps persist: government offices experience slow, intermittent internet connectivity, broadband penetration in rural areas remains limited, and the digital divide between urban centers and geographically isolated areas is pronounced.

    Workshop participants from the Infrastructure sector identified ICT infrastructure as a critical investment area, citing slow procurement, limited service provider presence, and power instability as root causes. Investment in digital infrastructure — fiber optic networks, wireless solutions, digital skills development facilities, and e-commerce platforms for MSMEs — is essential for unlocking productivity gains across all sectors.

    2.7 Socio-political scenarios, 2026–2035

    The investment case for BARMM cannot be separated from its political settlement. Three structural facts frame every scenario below.

    • A new political order. The appointed Bangsamoro Transition Authority hands over to the region's first elected Parliament on 30 October 2026. Polling on 14 September 2026 was described by COMELEC as generally peaceful with roughly 80 percent turnout, but it was preceded by an attempted ambush of the Interim Chief Minister in August and marked by localised disruptions, including blocked delivery of election materials in Al-Barka, Basilan.
    • A smaller, non-contiguous territory. Without Sulu, the island provinces of Basilan and Tawi-Tawi are separated from the mainland core and from each other. Connectivity — shipping, air links, power and digital — becomes a condition for governing the region as one economy, not a convenience.
    • A fiscal re-basing. Territorial change affects the population and revenue bases used for planning and intergovernmental transfers. Until re-based statistics and transfer formulas are settled, regional targets carry wider uncertainty bands.

    The three scenarios are not forecasts. They describe plausible paths so that investors and planners can identify early signals and pre-commit responses. No numeric probabilities are assigned; the evidence base does not support them.

    Planning basis. This roadmap uses Scenario B as its reference case. Early signals — a completed election with high turnout, alongside documented security incidents — are consistent with a functioning but contested transition. Targets in Chapter 5 should be reviewed after the elected Parliament's first twelve months, and the scenario judgement updated against the signposts above.

    3.1 The paradigm shift

    The draft workshops repeatedly described the same underlying problem from different angles: resources leave the region unprocessed, programmes are delivered in silos, and investment is attracted with incentives but not retained by institutions. BIRD 2026–2035 asks for a shift in the operating logic of the regional economy, summarised in the figure below.

    The shift matters for investors as much as for government. Each move on the right-hand side reduces a specific category of risk — supply risk, coordination risk, policy risk and execution risk — rather than adding return through subsidy. That is the more durable way to close the gap between BARMM's risk premium and its peers'.

    3.2 Regional Economic Structure (2024)

    In 2024, BARMM's economy was predominantly Services-based at 42.0 percent (₱125.7 billion), followed by Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing at 32.4 percent (₱97.2 billion), and Industry at 25.6 percent (₱76.6 billion). The Industry sector posted the strongest growth at 8.7 percent in 2024, led by construction (26.0 percent) and professional and business services (12.5 percent). AFF contracted by 4.2 percent due to El Niño impacts and disease outbreaks in rubber plantations.

    3.3 Lanao del Sur: BARMM's Fastest-Growing Province

    Lanao del Sur is the second-largest provincial economy in BARMM, contributing approximately 25.8 percent of the region's GDP. Its GDP reached ₱75.38 billion in 2023, reflecting a 5.02 percent real growth rate — the highest among all BARMM provinces in that year. In 2024, Lanao del Sur sustained 4.0 percent growth, the second-highest in the region.

    The province's economic structure has undergone meaningful transformation: Services at 38 percent (₱28.95B), Industry at 33 percent (₱24.79B, anchored by utilities and manufacturing), and AFF at 29 percent (₱21.64B, growing 8.7 percent in 2023 driven by the SPDA Agro-Industrial Development Program).

    3.4 Basilan: The Peace Dividend in Action

    Basilan's declaration as Abu Sayyaf Group (ASG)-free in June 2024 — with remaining ASG members surrendering in December 2024 — represents the most significant security milestone in the province's modern history. This has created favorable conditions for investment normalization. The province recorded GDP of ₱23.15 billion in 2023 (2.9 percent growth) and 1.6 percent growth in 2024.

    Basilan is the Philippines' largest rubber-producing province, with approximately 48,366 hectares planted to rubber trees. The ₱195 million BARMM rubber industry development program (2024) and the landmark ₱6.67 billion Zamboanga-Basilan Interconnection Project (ZBIP) — connecting Basilan to the Mindanao grid — are transforming the investment landscape.

    Infrastructure momentum is building: the ₱627 million MPW project portfolio for 2025 (90–100 percent completion rate), the proposed Basilan-Zamboanga Bridge projected for completion by 2030, and 40 completed infrastructure projects in 2024 are cumulatively reducing the historically formidable cost-of-doing-business in the province.

    3.6 The Bangsamoro Economic and Investment Ecosystem (BEIE) Framework

    The Bangsamoro Economic and Investment Ecosystem (BEIE) Framework, developed by Silva (2026), reframes investment analysis away from isolated sectoral silos toward an integrated view of how economic clusters interact and reinforce each other. The framework is grounded in systems-based economic theory and organizes BARMM's economic landscape into five functional clusters, anchored by Moral Governance.

    3.7 BEIE Applied: Green Economy as the Sixth Cluster

    This BIRD 2026–2035 extends the BEIE Framework by formally incorporating a Green Economy cluster, reflecting the convergence of several emerging policy and market developments that were not fully addressed in the original framework:

    • BIMP-EAGA EGL Sub-Committee Strategic Plan 2025–2028: Frames BARMM's environmental assets — forests, marine ecosystems, water resources — as drivers of regional economic value through eco-corridors, sustainable fisheries, and environmental governance.

    • Bangsamoro Forestry Code (pending enactment): Institutionalizes growth through sustainable resource utilization, value-added processing of timber and non-timber forest products (NTFPs), and access to emerging environmental markets.

    • Draft MENRE-MILG Joint Memorandum Circular No. 2026-01: Establishes frameworks for Carbon Credit Programs, Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES), and Eco-tourism User Fees as concrete revenue mechanisms for local government units and communities.

    • BARMM Plantation Establishment & Biodiversity Conservation Roadmap: A technical pathway to wood self-sufficiency, REDD+ carbon monetization, forest nursery enterprise development, and LGU-led economic zones.

    3.8 Strat Planner Pro Framework

    The Strat Planner Pro framework (Silva & Unsi, 2026) provides the strategic planning architecture for this roadmap. It integrates five analytical layers:

    • Layer 1 — SWOT Analysis: Comprehensive identification of internal strengths and weaknesses, external opportunities and threats, with stakeholder attribution and variable classification.

    • Layer 2 — Dynamic SWOT and Systems Thinking: Causal loop diagrams (CLDs), reinforcing and balancing feedback loops, and systems archetypes (Limits to Growth, Success to the Successful, Fixes that Fail) that reveal how SWOT elements interact over time.

    • Layer 3 — Strategy Matrix: Matching strategies to SWOT combinations (SO, ST, WO, WT) and identifying highest-leverage intervention points.

    • Layer 4 — Metrics and KPI Benchmarking: Aligned with OIC/SMIIC international standards, ESG frameworks, Philippine Development Plan 2023–2028 indicators, and Bangsamoro-specific KPIs.

    • Layer 5 — Balanced Scorecard: Translating strategy into actionable objectives, measures, targets, and initiatives across Financial, Stakeholder/Customer, Internal Process, and Learning & Growth perspectives.

    Chapter 4: Priority Investment Sectors and the Green Economy

    Six sectors and an emerging green pillar, read through a risk-return lens.

    Based on workshop consultations (Workshops 1–5), the BEIE Framework analysis, and provincial economic profiles, six priority investment sectors are identified for the BIRD 2026–2035. Each sector analysis integrates challenges, opportunities, strategic options, SWOT insights, and investment recommendations.

    4.1 A risk-return lens on the priority sectors

    Capital weighs expected return against the probability and severity of loss. In a post-conflict region, investors add a premium for governance, security and information risk on top of ordinary project risk. The roadmap's task is to compress that premium through institutions, information and de-risking instruments, not to inflate returns through incentives that expire — the "Fixes that Fail" pattern described in Chapter 7.

    The table gives indicative characteristics for each priority sector, based on typical project profiles. It is an analytical aid for sequencing, not an investment recommendation or a return forecast.

    4.2 Agro-Industry and Halal Value Chains

    Situation Analysis

    The agro-industry sector is BARMM's most strategically significant and most underperforming sector. Agriculture largely remains confined to low-value primary production, with limited agro-processing, weak market linkages, and post-harvest losses of 20–40 percent for key commodities. No major agro-processing plants existed in the region until the 2022 commissioning of DoleFil's ₱306M pineapple facility in Wao, Lanao del Sur — an anchor investment that demonstrates transformative potential.

    The sector's AFF GDP contracted 4.2 percent in 2024 due to El Niño impacts and Pestalotiopsis rubber fungal disease in Basilan — highlighting climate and disease vulnerabilities. However, structural momentum is building: the SPDA Agro-Industrial Development Program in Wao and Amai Manabilang is attracting foreign investment (Green Arrow Agri-Ventures, 499 jobs), MAFAR's provincial assembly programs are distributing financial subsidies and providing farm profiling, and BARMM is building UAE halal export corridors for agri and fisheries products.

    Key Investment Opportunities

    Workshop Recommendations (Workshop 1 & 2 Outputs)

    Participants identified the following priority actions for agro-industry investment:

    • Synchronized implementation of BMOAs' programs — MAFAR, SPDA, MTIT, MPW — rather than fragmented delivery

    • Institutionalization of the Bangsamoro Corporation (Bangsamoro Corp) as an anchor financing vehicle for agro-industry

    • Modernization of agro-industry machinery and equipment through PPP frameworks

    • Establishment of modern pre- and post-harvest facilities in all provinces

    • Strengthening marketing linkages with national and international manufacturers through trade roadshows

    • Provision and improvement of farm-to-market roads as prerequisite for investment viability

    4.3 Halal Economy

    Global Context and BARMM's Position

    The global halal market is estimated at USD 2.3 trillion, growing at 6–8 percent annually, with the ASEAN halal market at USD 1.38 trillion (MTIT target: capture 30 percent). BARMM's Muslim-majority population, cultural authority in Islamic practice, and existing agricultural raw material base create a natural ecosystem for halal production, certification, and export — a first-mover advantage that no other Philippine region can credibly replicate.

    Investment Architecture

    • Halal Governance and Regulatory Strengthening: Operationalize the Bangsamoro Halal Board (BHB) with OIC/SMIIC-aligned certification; harmonize standards with national and international frameworks; fast-track BHIDP 2025–2030 implementation. Short-term (Years 1–3).

    • Halal Ecosystem and Value Chain Development: Strengthen certification bodies; develop halal processing, logistics, and supply chain infrastructure; promote halal industry clustering (Halal Park in Matanog, Maguindanao del Norte). Medium-term (Years 1–7).

    • Islamic Finance as Sector Enabler: Promote Shariah-compliant MSME financing; develop Islamic microfinance pathways; expand fintech-enabled financial access linked to halal enterprise development. Long-term (Years 1–10).

    SWOT (Halal Sector — Workshop 4 Output)

    Strengths: Halal legitimacy and cultural credibility; strategic BIMP-EAGA location; strong AFF raw material base; growing policy recognition. Weaknesses: Weak certification system; fragmented policy framework; limited infrastructure; lack of halal experts. Opportunities: Expanding Philippine halal market; ASEAN and global halal economy growth; Islamic finance ecosystem development. Threats: Competition from Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand; standards recognition risks; investment perception risks; climate and production vulnerabilities.

    4.4 Tourism and Cultural Economy

    Situation Analysis

    Tourism is BARMM's most underdeveloped cross-cutting driver relative to its endowments. The region has significant potential: Lake Lanao eco-tourism; Maranao cultural heritage (torogan ancestral houses, okir design tradition, malong weaving); Yakan traditional textiles; Tausug and Bajau maritime culture; and the narrative of post-conflict regeneration in Marawi City.

    Investment Opportunities

    • Eco-Tourism and Nature-Based Tourism: Lake Lanao eco-tourism facilities; marine sanctuaries in Basilan and Tawi-Tawi; forest wilderness trails (consistent with Bangsamoro Forestry Code and EGL Sub-Committee framework)

    • Cultural and Heritage Tourism: Torogan conservation and heritage circuits; Maranao culinary and craft tourism; Master-Apprentice Bridge program for intergenerational knowledge transfer of traditional weaving

    • Halal and Muslim-Friendly Tourism: Halal-certified hotels, restaurants, and tourism facilities; prayer facility infrastructure; family-friendly Muslim-oriented travel packages

    • Tourism Infrastructure: Farm-tourism roads; port and wharf development; digital booking and promotion platforms; MSME value chain upgrading in tourism services

    Workshop Strategy (Workshop 5 Tourism Output)

    Strategic Focus Areas: (1) Eco-tourism — sustainable tourism leveraging natural landscapes; (2) Cultural Tourism — preserve and celebrate enriched heritage and indigenous traditions; (3) Halal/Muslim-Friendly Tourism — Islamic principle-compliant offerings attracting domestic and international Muslim travelers.

    Priority Programs: Tourism Infrastructure Support Program; Master-Apprentice Heritage Continuity Training; Tourism Product and Market Development; Tourism Destination Development; Tourism Investment Promotion; Tourism Governance and Sustainability.

    4.5 Energy and Utilities

    Situation Analysis

    Reliable, affordable energy is the single most important enabling condition for industrial investment in BARMM. The Workshop 1 Energy sector analysis identified three core problems: (1) unreliable electric power industry — ailing cooperatives, insufficient supply, poor distribution infrastructure; (2) insufficient electrification levels — 25–30 percent of households in remote areas lack access; and (3) unutilized renewable and non-renewable energy resources.

    The Bangsamoro Sustainable Energy Master Plan (BSEMP) envisions total investments reaching ₱11–19 billion by 2050 depending on the economic growth scenario. The Agus hydropower cascade in Lanao del Sur supplies a historically significant share of Mindanao's electricity. BARMM's renewable energy mix already stands at 75.86 percent, primarily hydropower.

    Investment Priorities

    • Grid Interconnection: Complete the ₱6.67B Zamboanga-Basilan Interconnection Project (ZBIP); extend reliable grid or hybrid supply to Tawi-Tawi

    • Hydropower Rehabilitation: Restore full operational capacity of the Agus cascade; support new micro-hydro projects in upland municipalities

    • Solar Energy: Solar farms for agro-industrial facilities; rooftop solar for commercial users; hybrid solar-diesel mini-grids for island communities

    • Biomass Energy: Power plants utilizing rubber wood, coconut shells, and rice hulls from agro-processing by-products

    • Off-Grid and Mini-Grid Solutions: Renewable energy systems for remote islands and geographically isolated areas in Tawi-Tawi and the island municipalities of Basilan

    Workshop Strategy (Workshop 5 Energy Output)

    Focus Area 1: Effective management of the electric power industry — improved distribution networks, sufficient energy supply, improved quality of life. Main Sensitivities: Regulatory transition, financial viability of ailing cooperatives.

    Focus Area 2: Exploration, development, and utilization of potential renewable and non-renewable energy resources; promotion of energy investment opportunities. Sensitivities: Geographic and technical isolation, institutional capacity gaps.

    Focus Area 3: Effective and efficient enabling policies; institutionalization of co-management mechanisms between national and Bangsamoro governments; strengthened partnerships with private sector, CSOs, and international partners.

    4.6 Infrastructure, Logistics, and Digital Connectivity

    Situation Analysis

    Infrastructure gaps are the most pervasively cited investment constraint across all BARMM sectors. The Workshop 1 Infrastructure analysis identified key problems: slow, limited internet connectivity in government offices; lack of classroom and WASH facilities; delays in project implementation due to right-of-way and peace-and-order conditions; insufficient ICT infrastructure investment in remote areas; incomplete trunk infrastructure in housing growth areas; and limited access to public internet in remote barangays.

    Investment Opportunities

    • Farm-to-Market Roads: Connecting agricultural production zones to markets and processing facilities — identified as the single highest-impact intervention by agro-industry workshop participants

    • Basilan-Zamboanga Bridge: Projected for completion by 2030 — transformative for Basilan's economic integration with mainland Mindanao

    • Port and Wharf Development: Upgrading existing ports; developing new wharves in key inter-island trade nodes in Tawi-Tawi and Basilan

    • Digital Infrastructure: Fiber optic networks; wireless broadband solutions; digital capacity development facilities; last-mile connectivity for government offices and schools

    • Warehousing and Cold Storage: Modern facilities in Tawi-Tawi (₱100M pipeline project), Maguindanao del Norte, and Lanao del Sur

    • Affordable Housing with Trunk Infrastructure: PPP models bundling roads, water, and power in pre-identified housing investment corridors; Shariah-compliant housing finance

    4.7 Services — Health, Education, Financial, and Digital

    Investment Context

    The Services sector leads BARMM's economy at 42.0 percent of GRDP (₱125.7 billion) and posted 5.1 percent growth in 2024. Within services, wholesale and retail trade (7.5% growth), professional and business services (12.5%), human health (10.9%), and financial/insurance activities (7.9%) were the fastest-growing subsectors.

    Priority Investment Areas

    • Healthcare: Tertiary-level private hospitals and diagnostic centers in provincial hubs (Basilan and Tawi-Tawi have high unmet demand); solar-powered cold chain storage for vaccines and medicines; telemedicine for island communities; Ibadah-Friendly health facilities integrating Islamic clinical care principles (BaSulTa Health Link Project)

    • Islamic Finance: Expansion of Islamic banking branches in key municipalities; Islamic microfinance and cooperative development; Shariah-compliant agricultural credit and takaful insurance; digital fintech solutions for underserved areas

    • Digital Services and E-Commerce: Digital skills training programs; e-commerce platforms for MSMEs; BEGMP e-governance implementation; digital literacy and coding courses aligned with growing demand for content creation, virtual assistance, and digital work

    • Education: Resolution of land ownership issues for donated school lots; ICT-equipped learning facilities; TESDA programs aligned with agro-industry, halal, and renewable energy employer needs; scholarships for Islamic finance and halal certification professionals

    4.8 Green Economy and Environmental Markets

    The Green Economy cluster represents the most strategically differentiated and globally aligned new investment frontier for BARMM in the 2026–2035 decade. While halal, agro-industry, and infrastructure have been identified as investment priorities for years, the convergence of four key policy and market developments now makes the Green Economy a concrete and revenue-generating investment pathway — not merely an aspirational sustainability goal.

    BIMP-EAGA EGL Sub-Committee Strategic Plan 2025–2028

    The BIMP-EAGA Environment, Green Economy, and Land Use (EGL) Sub-Committee Strategic Plan 2025–2028 establishes a regional framework for translating BARMM's environmental endowments into economic value. As the Philippines' BIMP-EAGA member with the largest forest cover in insular Southeast Asia within the Growth Area, BARMM has a distinctive role in the EGL agenda.

    The EGL Strategic Plan identifies four regional economic drivers relevant to BARMM:

    • Eco-Corridors and Biodiversity Conservation: Transboundary protected areas, wildlife corridors, and marine sanctuaries as both conservation tools and eco-tourism foundations

    • Sustainable Fisheries and Aquaculture: Harmonized standards for cross-border halal fisheries products; seaweed farming as a BIMP-EAGA priority commodity

    • Environmental Governance Mechanisms: Carbon market development, PES frameworks, and environmental monitoring systems across BIMP-EAGA member areas

    • Green Infrastructure: Climate-resilient infrastructure design standards applicable to BARMM's road, water, and energy investments

    Bangsamoro Forestry Code — Institutionalizing Green Growth

    The Bangsamoro Forestry Code, currently before the ENRE Committee under the chairpersonship of MP Tawakkal Midtimbang, represents the most significant piece of environmental legislation in BARMM's history. When enacted, it will establish the legal architecture for:

    • Sustainable Timber Industry: Licensing and monitoring of timber production; establishment of community forestry programs; wood processing enterprises targeting self-sufficiency in construction materials

    • Non-Timber Forest Products (NTFPs): Commercial production of rattan, bamboo, honey, medicinal plants, and resins under community-based management agreements

    • Value-Added Forest Products Processing: Furniture manufacturing, wooden handicrafts, bamboo products — aligned with halal and cultural tourism sectors

    • Forest Nurseries as Economic Enterprises: LGU-managed and community-managed nurseries supplying reforestation programs, generating income while restoring forest cover

    • Environmental Market Access: Legal framework establishing BARMM's standing to participate in carbon credit programs and international environmental payment mechanisms

    Draft MENRE-MILG JMC No. 2026-01: Revenue Mechanisms for LGUs

    The Draft MENRE-MILG Joint Memorandum Circular No. 2026-01 — currently for signature — is the operational gateway to environmental revenue generation for BARMM's local government units. It establishes three concrete frameworks:

    Carbon Credit Programs

    The JMC provides the regulatory basis for BARMM LGUs and community associations to participate in voluntary and compliance carbon markets. Forest conservation and reforestation activities that prevent emissions can be quantified, verified, and sold as carbon credits to international buyers — including corporations with net-zero commitments and climate finance funds.

    BARMM's forest resources — estimated at millions of hectares across Lanao del Sur, the Maguindanao provinces, Basilan and Tawi-Tawi — represent a massive potential carbon stock. Conservative estimates suggest that a well-managed REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation) program for BARMM could generate hundreds of millions of pesos annually for LGUs and forest-dependent communities, with revenue streams lasting decades.

    Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES)

    PES mechanisms compensate landowners, communities, and LGUs for maintaining ecosystems that provide services to downstream users — water supply, flood control, biodiversity habitat, climate regulation. The JMC establishes frameworks for:

    • Watershed PES: Payments from water utilities, irrigators, and industrial water users to upland communities maintaining forest cover that protects water catchments — highly relevant for Lake Lanao, the Cotabato River Basin, and island aquifers in Tawi-Tawi and Basilan

    • Coastal PES: Payments from tourism operators, fishing communities, and aquaculture enterprises to communities maintaining mangroves, seagrass beds, and coral reefs

    • Carbon PES: Integration with carbon credit programs — combining biodiversity and carbon co-benefits into higher-value ecosystem service payments

    Eco-Tourism User Fees

    The JMC establishes the regulatory framework for LGUs and protected area management boards to collect eco-tourism user fees from visitors to natural sites. Revenue is allocated to conservation management, community livelihood programs, and infrastructure maintenance — creating a virtuous cycle where tourism success funds the conservation that makes tourism viable.

    Integration with BEIE Framework

    The Green Economy cluster is not a separate pillar but an integral layer of the BEIE Framework:

    • Foundations: Healthy forests, watersheds, and marine ecosystems are the ecological foundations of AFF production — degraded resources undermine the entire production base

    • Transformers: Bamboo, rattan, and timber processing transform forest resources into value-added products for construction, furniture, and handicrafts

    • Enablers: Green infrastructure (climate-resilient roads, solar-powered facilities) enables production in environmentally challenging contexts

    • Connectors: Carbon credits, PES payments, and eco-tourism fees connect BARMM's environmental assets to global financial flows

    • Financiers: Green bonds, climate finance facilities (GCF, GEF), and ESG-aligned Islamic finance instruments channel capital to environmental investments

    • Cross-Cutting: Environmental stewardship embedded in Moral Governance — stewardship (khalifa) of the earth is an Islamic ethical imperative, aligning green economy with BARMM's cultural identity

    Chapter 6: Implementation Roadmap and Policy Recommendations

    Three phases, the conditions for success, and what each audience should do.

    The BIRD 2026–2035 is implemented in three phases aligned with BARMM's governance milestones and sectoral investment readiness:

    6.1 Phase 1: Foundation Building (2026–2028)

    The initial phase focuses on establishing the enabling conditions without which production investment cannot scale: governance infrastructure, halal certification systems, digital business facilitation, energy connections, and the legal framework for the green economy.

    6.2 Phase 2: Acceleration (2029–2032)

    Phase 2 leverages the governance and infrastructure foundations of Phase 1 to scale investment across all priority sectors, expand value chains, and activate the green economy revenue streams.

    6.3 Phase 3: Consolidation and Global Integration (2033–2035)

    Phase 3 consolidates BARMM's position as a recognized halal, agro-industrial, and sustainable investment destination in the ASEAN region, with internationally benchmarked standards, diversified export markets, and self-sustaining economic momentum.

    6.4 Critical Success Factors

    • Political Commitment: Sustained leadership commitment to the Integrated Halal-Driven Investment Strategy across political transitions, including the transition to an elected Bangsamoro Parliament.

    • Resource Mobilization: Adequate funding from BARMM block grants, national government programs, Official Development Assistance (ODA), and private investment capital.

    • Inter-Agency Coordination: Synchronization across MAFAR, SPDA, MPW, MTIT, MENRE, BBOI, BEZA, and provincial LGUs — the greatest institutional challenge identified in all workshops.

    • Security and Stability: Continued progress in the Bangsamoro peace process; effective management of residual security challenges.

    • Monitoring and Evaluation: Quarterly review of KPIs; annual Balanced Scorecard assessment; mid-term review in 2030 to adjust Phase 3 priorities.

    6.5 For Policymakers

    The following recommendations are directed at the Bangsamoro Parliament, the Chief Minister's Office, and BARMM ministries:

    • Enact the Bangsamoro Forestry Code and sign MENRE-MILG JMC No. 2026-01 as priority legislation in 2026, establishing BARMM's green economy legal architecture before the carbon credit and PES markets are fully mature — first-mover advantage is time-sensitive.

    • Establish a dedicated Bangsamoro Investment Command Center within the MTIT-BOI, with real-time investment tracking, aftercare services, and cross-agency coordination authority, to serve as the operational hub for BIRD implementation.

    • Fast-track BHIDP 2025–2030 implementation — particularly BHB operational strengthening and halal certification infrastructure — as the halal governance system is the prerequisite for all other halal economy interventions.

    • Prioritize completion of Marawi City's post-conflict rehabilitation and the Most Affected Area (MAA) commercial infrastructure, to restore BARMM's primary urban economic engine and signal to investors that peace dividends are visible and permanent.

    • Develop a Bangsamoro Climate-Smart Agriculture Roadmap integrating MAFAR programs, SPDA agro-industrial initiatives, MENRE environmental management, and climate adaptation measures into a coherent provincial-level strategy for each BARMM province, Cotabato City and the SGA.

    • Institutionalize the Bangsamoro Corporation (Bangsamoro Corp) as the anchor financing vehicle for agro-industrial investments, providing a culturally appropriate and administratively credible vehicle for BARMM-owned industrial participation.

    • Accelerate Islamic banking licensing expansion to all provincial capitals, with Al-Amanah Islamic Investment Bank of the Philippines as anchor institution, to improve capital access for the 5.69 million Bangsamoro population.

    6.6 For Development and Investment Planners

    • Apply the BEIE Framework in all provincial investment planning exercises, mapping sectoral investments to the five clusters and explicitly identifying inter-cluster linkages rather than planning in silos.

    • Design investment priority lists that include the Green Economy cluster as a formal category, with concrete projects in carbon credits, PES, eco-tourism, sustainable timber, and NTFP processing assigned to specific provinces and municipalities.

    • Develop a BARMM Infrastructure Investment Sequencing Plan that explicitly orders infrastructure priorities based on production investment readiness: farm-to-market roads and cold chain first, port and bridge improvements second, digital connectivity third — ensuring that infrastructure leads production, not follows it.

    • Establish provincial investment facilitation offices in Lanao del Sur (Marawi City), Basilan (Lamitan City) and Tawi-Tawi (Bongao) as satellite offices of the one-stop action center for investments that the draft Bangsamoro Investment Code places under the MTIT-BOI, reducing geographic barriers to investment registration.

    6.7 For Investors

    The following investment opportunities offer the strongest combination of comparative advantage, policy support, and near-term returns:

    • Agro-industrial investors should target the Wao–Amai Manabilang corridor in Lanao del Sur — where SPDA program infrastructure, MAFAR extension services, a ₱306M anchor precedent (DoleFil), and foreign investment momentum (Green Arrow Agri-Ventures) create the most mature agro-industrial ecosystem in BARMM.

    • Halal food and consumer goods investors in Marawi City enjoy first-mover advantage: the city's Islamic cultural authority and Maranao food heritage create authentic differentiation in the global halal market that no other Philippine city can credibly replicate.

    • Rubber value chain investors should assess Basilan's 48,366-hectare plantation base and ₱195M BARMM government investment as signals of a sector with policy commitment and natural resource foundation — the missing component is modern processing technology.

    • Carbon and green finance investors have a time-sensitive window: BARMM's forests are large, its governance framework for carbon markets is being established now (JMC No. 2026-01), and first-movers who establish REDD+ programs in 2026–2028 will benefit from the highest-integrity credits and strongest community partnerships.

    • Islamic finance and fintech investors should recognize BARMM as one of the largest underserved Islamic finance markets in Southeast Asia — 5.69 million primarily Muslim residents with a growing economy, minimal formal banking penetration, and strong cultural alignment with Shariah-compliant products.

    • Renewable energy investors should focus on: (a) completing grid interconnection to Basilan and improving supply reliability in Tawi-Tawi; (b) solar installations for agro-processing facilities in Lanao del Sur and Maguindanao; and (c) biomass power plants utilizing rubber wood and coconut shells from agro-industrial by-products.

    • Tourism investors should conduct feasibility studies for Lake Lanao eco-tourism, Maranao cultural heritage circuits, and halal hospitality infrastructure in anticipation of peace dividend maturation and the completion of major road and bridge projects by 2030.

    7.2 SWOT Analysis

    The following comprehensive SWOT analysis synthesizes findings from workshop consultations (Workshops 1–5), provincial economic outlooks, and the macro-environmental scanning conducted in Chapter 2.

    7.3 Dynamic SWOT and Systems Thinking Analysis

    A static SWOT matrix reveals what the situation is; Systems Thinking reveals why it persists and how to change it. Drawing from the Strat Planner Pro framework and Senge's (1990) systems archetypes, the following feedback loops characterize BARMM's investment system dynamics.

    Reinforcing Loop R1: Investment-Development Virtuous Cycle

    When investments flow into priority sectors (halal, agro-industry, tourism), they generate employment and income growth, which expands the domestic consumer market, improves business viability, and attracts additional investors. This virtuous cycle is currently nascent — evidenced by the trajectory from ₱2.1B (2022) to ₱5.1B (Q1 2026) in approvals — but requires sustained catalyst investments to reach self-sustaining velocity.

    Leverage Point: Front-load investments in halal certification infrastructure, agro-processing facilities, and digital business registration to accelerate the virtuous cycle before momentum plateaus.

    Reinforcing Loop R2: Governance-Investor Confidence Cycle

    Improved governance (transparency, rule of law, investor protection) increases investor confidence, which drives investment, generates economic growth, expands the tax base, and provides resources for further governance improvements. The first elected Bangsamoro Parliament, seated on 30 October 2026, is the critical juncture in this loop: a stable first term will amplify confidence; legislative paralysis or contested legitimacy would reverse it.

    Balancing Loop B1: Growth-Resource Constraints

    Rapid investment growth may encounter balancing pressures from infrastructure bottlenecks (unreliable energy, poor roads), skills shortages (59.3% literacy rate), and environmental limits (forest degradation, water stress). The "Limits to Growth" archetype suggests that without proactive investment in enabling infrastructure and human capital, growth will plateau as the system hits resource ceilings.

    Leverage Point: Infrastructure-first investments — especially the ₱6.67B Zamboanga-Basilan Interconnection Project, Marawi commercial rehabilitation, and digital connectivity — must precede or accompany production investment, not follow it.

    Reinforcing Loop R3: Security–Investment Tensions

    Security incidents — even isolated rido or armed group activity — trigger investor caution, reducing investment flows and slowing development that would otherwise contribute to stability. This loop highlights the critical importance of sustaining and communicating BARMM's security improvements to break negative narrative cycles.

    Note on polarity. The draft labelled this loop B2 (balancing). Its links — development reduces incidents, incidents reduce confidence, confidence raises investment — multiply to a positive sign, which makes it reinforcing: it amplifies whichever direction it is pushed. That is why a single high-profile incident can undo months of promotion, and why sustained security gains compound. Leverage Point: publish verified, location-specific security data so that investors price risk by corridor rather than by region.

    Systems Archetype: "Fixes that Fail"

    Workshop participants noted that ad hoc tax incentives without systemic governance reform may attract initial investments but fail to build institutional capacity for sustained attraction. The pattern: short-term relief from incentives → delayed systemic reform → institutional weakness persists → investors leave when incentives expire.

    Leverage Point: Pair all investment incentives with institutional capacity-building requirements — halal certification, digital business registration, one-stop shop expansion — to avoid the "Fixes that Fail" trap.

    7.4 The case for a connected BARMM

    The redrawn territory makes one argument unavoidable. Lanao del Sur, the Maguindanao provinces, Cotabato City and the SGA form a contiguous mainland core; Basilan and Tawi-Tawi are island provinces reached only by sea or air. An investment roadmap for this region is therefore also a connectivity roadmap. Halal supply chains need cold chain links between island producers and mainland processors; tourism needs scheduled transport; certification and business registration need digital channels that work from Bongao as well as from Cotabato City.

    A connected BARMM is not an aspiration added to the economic case — it is the economic case. Every reinforcing loop in this chapter runs faster when goods, power, data and people move reliably between the parts of the region, and every balancing loop bites harder when they do not.

    7.5 Conclusion

    The Bangsamoro Autonomous Region stands at a genuine inflection point. The data is clear: investment approvals have surpassed ₱5.1 billion in the first four months of 2026; poverty has fallen from 55.9 percent (2018) to 34.8 percent (2023); Lanao del Sur is BARMM's fastest-growing province; Basilan has achieved the historic milestone of being declared ASG-free; and the region ranks in the top 10 nationally for investment approvals.

    But data trends do not determine destinies. The BIRD 2026–2035 is premised on the conviction that the difference between scattered progress and synchronized ecosystem development is decisive for a region with as much potential as BARMM. The BEIE Framework's core insight — that sectors are deeply interconnected, and treating them as isolated leads to inefficiencies and missed opportunities — is not merely an analytical observation; it is a governance imperative.

    The ten-year investment vision articulated in this roadmap is ambitious but achievable:

    • A ₱550 billion economy by 2035 — nearly double the current GRDP

    • ₱15 billion in annual investment approvals — tripling the current level

    • 20,000+ jobs created by BBOI-registered enterprises over the decade

    • Poverty incidence below 20 percent — meaningful progress toward the national rate

    • BARMM recognised as a premier halal, agro-industrial and sustainable investment destination in Southeast Asia, starting from defensible niches

    • A functioning green economy generating ₱500M+ annually from carbon credits and PES

    • 5,000+ MSMEs with halal certification and access to Shariah-compliant finance

    Achieving this vision requires three non-negotiable conditions: political commitment sustained across governance transitions; inter-agency coordination that replaces siloed program delivery with ecosystem thinking; and investment in human capital that treats BARMM's 59.3 percent functional literacy rate not as an afterthought but as the binding constraint it is.

    The peace dividend is real. The investment momentum is building. The regulatory architecture — Bangsamoro Forestry Code, JMC No. 2026-01, BHIDP 2025–2030, BIMP-EAGA EGL framework — is falling into place. What remains is the decisive will to act with the discipline, coordination, and ambition that the moment demands.

    The Bangsamoro is no longer merely a recovery story. It is an emerging investment destination — one that can, in the decade ahead, prove that a post-conflict, predominantly Muslim, ecosystem-rich region can achieve sustainable, inclusive, and ethically grounded economic growth. The BIRD 2026–2035 is the roadmap for that journey.
