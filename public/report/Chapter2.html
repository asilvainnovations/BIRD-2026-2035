Context, BEIE Framework and Integrated Strategic Diagnostics

From Environmental Scanning to Systems Sensing

A conventional environmental scan lists political, economic, social, environmental and technological factors one after another. That is useful but incomplete, because in BARMM these factors do not act separately. A drought in Maguindanao del Sur is an environmental event, but it becomes an economic shock to processors, a fiscal pressure on local governments and a social strain on farming households within one season. This chapter therefore uses the Bangsamoro Economic and Investment Ecosystem (BEIE) Framework as a living systems lens: it asks not only what is happening in each domain, but where each force acts within the economy and how it travels between clusters (Silva, 2026).

Deep presencing means sensing both the visible and the emergent: the data and trends, and also the relationships, feedback loops, institutional habits and cultural currents that produce them (Senge, 1990; Meadows, 2008). The chapter moves from context (Sections 2.1 and 2.2), through a quantified diagnosis of the cross-cutting operating systems and the five clusters (Sections A to F), to a synthesis of how the clusters behave as one system (Section G).

2.1 Global Economic Trends: Implications for BARMM

The global outlook is steady but fragile. The International Monetary Fund projects world growth of 3.1 percent in 2026 and 3.4 percent in 2027 (IMF, 2026). For BARMM, the external environment matters less through headline growth than through specific channels: the price of food, fuel and fertiliser; the direction of regional supply chains; the availability of climate and Islamic finance; and the pace of digital change. 2.2 Regional Economic Trajectory: Indicators and Inflection Points

BARMM's trajectory shows real progress on three fronts: output, investment and poverty (Figure 2-2). Growth slowed to 2.7 percent in 2024, after 4.3 percent in 2023 and 6.6 percent in 2022 (PSA-RSSO BARMM, 2024, 2025a), then recovered to 5.0 percent in 2025, the sixth fastest of the country's 18 regions (MindaNews, 2026). Investment approvals have accelerated, and poverty has fallen sharply since 2018.

Approvals are a signal of intent, not of capital on the ground. They have also been volatile: ₱6.97 billion was approved in 2021, followed by lower totals before the recent rise (MTIT, 2026a). The roadmap therefore tracks realised investment, jobs and retention alongside approvals (Chapter 4).

Economic structure

The structure of the economy explains why value is lost between clusters. Agriculture, forestry and fishing (AFF) generate about a third of regional output, roughly four times their share nationally (about 8 percent), yet most of that output leaves the region unprocessed (Figure 2-3). The gap between a large Foundations cluster and a small Transformers cluster is the clearest structural signal in the regional accounts.

Inflection points

Governance transition. The first regular Bangsamoro Parliament was elected on 14 September 2026, with turnout of about 80 percent. No party won a majority, so the Chief Minister will be chosen through coalition when members take office after 30 October 2026 (Reuters, 2026). A peaceful election strengthens governance credibility; a contested coalition process would test it. The campaign also saw an ambush on the interim Chief Minister on 15 August 2026, who survived the attack (Daily Tribune, 2026), a reminder that political stability still needs active protection.

Security normalisation. Basilan was declared free of the Abu Sayyaf Group on 9 June 2025 (PIA, 2025). For investors, this lowers the risk premium in a province with large rubber and aquaculture endowments and a ₱6.67 billion power interconnection project under way (MTIT, 2026b).

A. Cross-Cutting Operating Systems: Moral Governance, Resilience, Inclusivity and Peace

A.1 Functional Descriptions and Deep Presencing Indicators

The BEIE Framework treats moral governance, resilience, inclusivity and peace as the operating systems of the economy, not as aspirations. As an operating system manages how hardware and software interact, these four systems govern the trust, incentives and accountability through which value is created in every cluster (Silva, 2026). The political domain of PEDSET acts mainly here, together with the social and environmental domains through inclusivity and resilience.

The workshops repeatedly identified three bottlenecks: fragmented programme delivery across Bangsamoro ministries, offices and agencies (BMOAs); weak inter-agency coordination that produces duplication and gaps; and investor hesitation rooted in perceptions of governance and security risk. These are not sectoral failures. They are operating-system failures, and they call for changes in rules, information flows and institutional design rather than more sector programmes (Meadows, 1999; BoI-MTIT, 2026).

Security risk is uneven across the region. Clan feuds (rido), residual armed groups and disputes over land and resources are the main drivers on the mainland; maritime insecurity and smuggling are concerns in the island provinces. These patterns are monitored by the security sector and independent conflict monitors, and the roadmap uses them as risk signals rather than as fixed ratings. The most effective response is to keep normalisation programmes funded, invest in community-led peacebuilding and publish security and investment data together, so that improvements reach investors as evidence rather than as assertion.

Basilan illustrates the peace–investment link. Its declaration as free of the Abu Sayyaf Group on 9 June 2025 came as the ₱6.67 billion Zamboanga–Basilan Interconnection Project and major public works were advancing in the province (PIA, 2025; MTIT, 2026b). Security, power and logistics improvements reinforce one another; none of them alone would attract processing investment.

A.4 Environmental and Geophysical Hazards

The provinces, the Special Geographic Area and Cotabato City face earthquakes, flooding, landslides, storm surges and drought. Climate change is the second-highest-rated threat in the survey (VI 3.14), and the evidence shows it is already a present cost: El Niño-induced drought led Maguindanao del Sur to declare a state of calamity in 2024, with ₱346 million in crop losses across 1,370 hectares, and Pestalotiopsis leaf disease has infested at least 800 hectares of Basilan's rubber (MTIT, 2026b).

A.5 Deep Presencing Insight: Applying the Iceberg Model

The operating systems must be strengthened first. Incentives offered without parallel governance reform can produce short-term approvals but not lasting transformation. The Iceberg Model explains why: visible events are produced by deeper patterns, structures and mental models (Senge, 1990; Meadows, 1999). Stakeholders strongly endorsed this reading: 85 percent agreed that the Iceberg Model describes BARMM's investment system, with a mean rating of 4.26 out of 5 (BIRD Validation Survey, 2026).

Level 1: Events (what is happening?)
Visible outcomes that trigger immediate responses: approvals rising to ₱5.1 billion in January–April 2026; capital that arrives with incentive announcements and leaves when incentive periods end; budget execution below 80 percent. Reacting only to events treats symptoms.
Level 2: Patterns (what keeps recurring?)
Investment surges followed by slowdowns; persistent gaps between policy announcement and implementation; recurring delays in procurement, certification and aftercare. Isolated interventions produce temporary gains without changing the trajectory.

Level 3: Structures (what produces the patterns?)
Incentives decoupled from institutional capacity; programme delivery fragmented across BMOAs; no real-time system linking governance performance to investment outcomes; weak feedback from investor experience to policy. Structures decide whether incentives compound into growth or dissipate through friction.
Level 4: Mental models (what beliefs hold the system in place?)
The belief that fiscal incentives alone attract quality investment; the assumption that governance reform can follow production rather than precede it; the view that sectors work independently; and the habit of measuring success by approvals rather than retention, impact and inclusion. Shifting from "incentives first" to "governance first" changes the whole strategy.

The Foundations cluster is the resource base on which the rest of the economy depends. B.2 Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry: The Living Resource Base

Agro-industry is the region's most strategic and most under-performing sector. Production remains concentrated in low-value primary goods, with limited processing, weak market links and high post-harvest losses. Anchor investments show what is possible: DoleFil's ₱306 million pineapple packing plant in Wao began commercial operation in 2022 and employs more than 1,200 people, and Green Arrow Agri-Ventures' foreign-owned project in Amai Manabilang, under the SPDA programme, is projected to create 499 jobs (MTIT, 2026b).

Climate and disease are already testing the base. AFF output contracted 4.2 percent in 2024 (PSA-RSSO BARMM, 2025a), as El Niño drought hit Maguindanao del Sur and Pestalotiopsis spread through Basilan's rubber. The provinces' endowments differ sharply (Figures 2-6 and 2-7; Table 2-12), so a single regional programme would fit none of them well.

B.3 Energy and Environmental Systems: Powering and Protecting the Base

Reliable, affordable power is the single most important enabling condition for industrial investment. The generation mix is predominantly renewable, but access is not: about 40 percent of potential household connections in BARMM electric cooperative areas were energised in 2023 (NEA, 2023), and region-wide household electrification stood at 67.52 percent at the end of 2025 (Context.ph, 2026). Island provinces rely heavily on diesel, which raises costs for processors. Workshop 1 identified three core energy problems: an unreliable power industry with ailing electric cooperatives; insufficient electrification in island and remote barangays; and unused renewable and non-renewable potential (BoI-MTIT, 2026). The devolution of power-sector functions from the DOE to MENRE, agreed in 2026, gives the region new tools and new responsibilities (Context.ph, 2026).

C. Cluster 2: Transformers — Industry, Manufacturing, Logistics and Halal Processing

The Transformers cluster is the value-adding engine: the processing and manufacturing capacity that turns Foundation materials into market-ready products. It decides whether BARMM captures value within its borders or exports it as unprocessed commodities. Industry accounts for 25.6 percent of regional output (PSA-RSSO BARMM, 2025a), and manufacturing in Maguindanao del Norte grew 27 percent between 2018 and 2023 (MTIT, 2026b).

PEDSET signals in this cluster. Economic: scale, input costs and access to buyers. Political: halal certification governance and standards recognition. Technological: cold chain, quality laboratories and traceability. Social: cultural legitimacy of Bangsamoro halal products.
Transformers cluster: key evidence
• Industry share of GRDP: 25.6% (2024)

D. Cluster 3: Enablers — Infrastructure, Social Services and Protection, Health and Human Capital

The Enablers cluster is the physical, digital and human infrastructure that makes value creation possible. Without reliable power, transport, connectivity and skills, Foundations cannot reach Transformers and Transformers cannot reach Connectors. The quantified evidence identifies Enablers as the binding constraint: it has the highest weakness risk (17.13 out of 25), the lowest readiness (3.33) and the highest urgency (4.04) of all clusters (Table 2-5).

PEDSET signals in this cluster. Demographic: a young, fast-growing population. Social: poverty, literacy and malnutrition. Technological: broadband gaps and digital public services. Economic: infrastructure cost overruns and budget execution.

The human-capital gap is acute. In 2024, 81.0 percent of residents had basic literacy, the lowest of any region (national: 90.0 percent), and 64.7 percent were functionally literate (national: 70.8 percent) (PSA, 2025; PSA-RSSO BARMM, 2025b). The population is growing about 3.43 percent a year (MTIT, 2026a). That growth becomes a demographic dividend only if education and skills keep pace with what employers need. Workshop 1 identified the skills mismatch, with technical and vocational programmes concentrated in a few oversubscribed qualifications, as a direct barrier to investment (BoI-MTIT, 2026).

E. Cluster 4: Connectors — Tourism, Digital Connectivity, Trade, Exports and BIMP-EAGA Integration

The Connectors cluster is how BARMM's products, services and cultural assets reach markets beyond the region. It determines whether value creation stays local or earns external revenue and multiplier effects. BARMM has a dual gateway: BIMP-EAGA to the south and west, through Tawi-Tawi, about 20 km from Sabah, and the Philippine domestic market to the north, through the mainland and the central Mindanao corridor (MTIT, 2026b). Ports, customs facilitation and digital trade platforms remain underdeveloped.

PEDSET signals in this cluster. Economic: halal market growth and competition from established hubs. Political: cross-border trade rules and security. Technological: logistics and digital trade. Social: cultural heritage as a tourism asset.

F. Cluster 5: Financiers — Islamic Banking, Waqf, Takaful and Microfinance

The Financiers cluster is the capital circulation system for all other clusters. In BARMM it has a distinctive dimension: Shariah-compliant finance fits the values of a predominantly Muslim population of about 4.5 million people, and offers a recognised route to global Islamic capital. Yet formal financial penetration is low, and Islamic banking assets in the region are estimated at below ₱2 billion (BIRD KPI register).

PEDSET signals in this cluster. Economic: MSME credit demand and project bankability. Political: national regulation and regional coordination. Social: religious values and financial literacy. Technological: digital financial services for island and rural users.

Republic Act No. 11439 provides the national framework for Islamic banking. The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas licenses and supervises Islamic banks; the region's role is to build demand, project pipelines, Shariah-governance capacity and public-sector use of Islamic instruments. The government-owned Al-Amanah Islamic Investment Bank of the Philippines operates in the region, and CARD Islamic Bank has established operations in Marawi City under RA 11439 (MTIT, 2026b). Stakeholders favour macro-capital instruments such as sukuk and infrastructure finance as the first priority (56 percent), and 72 percent consider a coordinating Islamic finance authority essential.

G. Cross-Cluster Systems Synthesis: From Five Diagnostics to One Investment System

The BEIE Framework's value lies less in describing each cluster than in showing how the clusters interact through reinforcing and balancing loops. These interactions decide whether the ecosystem compounds value or loses it through fragmentation and bottlenecks (Silva, 2026; Senge, 1990).

Cross-cluster systems synthesis

Two Engines of Ecosystem Growth

System sequencing. Foundations supply the productive and ecological base; Enablers lift capacity ceilings; Transformers convert resources into higher-value products; Connectors move them into domestic, ASEAN and global markets; Financiers mobilise capital across the system. The operating systems decide whether these flows are trusted, inclusive, resilient and peaceful.
Integrated investment logic. The evidence supports a sequencing principle rather than five separate sector programmes: secure the resource base → build enabling capacity → scale transformation → connect markets → deepen finance, with governance, resilience, inclusion and peace working at every step.

The throughput reality

Because value passes through the clusters in sequence, readiness gaps multiply rather than average. Table 2-16 makes the arithmetic explicit. Averaged, the clusters look about 69 percent ready; multiplied through the chain, the system converts only about 16 percent of its potential, and about 11.5 percent once the operating systems are applied. The weakest link, Enablers, caps the whole chain.

The master reinforcing loop (R6)
Foundations (AFF at 32.4% of GRDP) → Enablers (the ₱6.67B Zamboanga–Basilan Interconnection Project) → Transformers (manufacturing up 27% in Maguindanao del Norte, 2018–2023) → Connectors (BIMP-EAGA and GCC corridors) → Financiers (approvals of ₱5.1B in January–April 2026) → back to Foundations (anchor investors such as DoleFil in Wao sourcing from local farms).

Implication: synchronisation is essential, because weakness at any one point constrains the whole loop. This is the structural case for the Integrated Ecosystem Development Strategy recommended in Chapter 3.

Provincial diagnostics and island–mainland dynamics

The cluster analysis shows an island–mainland divergence. In the island provinces, Basilan and Tawi-Tawi, strong Foundation endowments (rubber, seaweed and fisheries) hit hard Enabler ceilings in power, shipping, connectivity and skills, the Limits to Growth pattern. On the mainland, the main constraint is institutional: interventions produce uneven results because of coordination and capacity gaps, the Fixes that Fail and Shifting the Burden patterns. The Special Geographic Area, with new local governments and unresolved land tenure, needs institutional and infrastructure investment at the same time.
